### pepper-pickled

**Pickled / vinegar-preserved pepper** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** Vinegar-preserved peppers carry the parent pepper's vitamin C and carotenoids (though heat and brine knock down the C considerably), plus the acetic acid of the brine itself, which has a modest blunting effect on post-meal glucose excursions in the literature. They're a flavour multiplier — sour, bright, a touch of heat — that lets a dish lean on fewer added fats and less salt elsewhere. Neither Longo nor Blueprint single them out, but they fit naturally inside a Mediterranean larder.

**How to consume.** Treat them as a finishing condiment, not a vegetable serving — a tablespoon chopped into a grain bowl, a few rings on a bean stew, a slice on a tartine. Drain before plating if the sodium load of the dish is already high. Pair with eggs, pulses, or oily fish; the acid cuts the fat and lifts the flavour. Once opened, refrigerate and use within a few weeks.

**When to consume.** Lunch and dinner work best — the acid pairs awkwardly with sweet breakfasts, and the salt isn't ideal late at night for blood-pressure-sensitive eaters. Daily use at small volumes is fine.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Diets without any pickled or fermented acidic foods miss the small post-meal-glucose benefit acetic acid provides, but the gap is trivial next to the parent vegetable intake.

**Excess signs.** The sodium from the brine is the only meaningful concern at high daily volumes — a half-jar a day stacks with other salt sources and can push blood pressure in cohort data. Otherwise no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### salt

**Salt** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Sodium chloride is genuinely essential — sodium drives nerve conduction, muscle contraction, and extracellular fluid balance, and chloride supports stomach acid. The body cannot make it. The question for longevity diets is never *whether* to eat salt but *how much*, and both Blueprint and Longo land in the same place: cook with restraint, lean on herbs and acid for flavour, avoid the heavily processed foods that supply the bulk of Western sodium intake.

**How to consume.** Add salt at the end of cooking where possible — you'll use less to hit the same perceived saltiness. Iodised salt remains the simplest hedge against iodine gaps in non-seafood-heavy diets. Flaky finishing salt on a finished dish is doing more flavour work per gram than the same salt dissolved into a stew. Lemon, vinegar, garlic, and fresh herbs all let you pull salt down without the dish tasting flat.

**When to consume.** With food, throughout the day. There is no time-of-day rule worth following at culinary doses.

**Deficiency signs.** Genuine sodium deficiency from diet alone is rare in industrial societies, but very-low-sodium diets combined with heavy sweating or endurance exertion can show up as muscle cramps, low-grade headaches, fatigue, and dizziness on standing.

**Excess signs.** This is the one entry where the excess side matters most. The typical Western diet sits roughly twice the level of traditional Mediterranean intake, and chronically high sodium tracks with hypertension across large cohort studies — particularly in salt-sensitive eaters. The biggest lever isn't the salt shaker; it's processed bread, cured meats, restaurant food, and packaged snacks. Cooking from raw ingredients, as both Blueprint and Longo do, brings sodium back to a sane range almost on its own.

### pepper-black

**Black pepper** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Black pepper's signature compound is **piperine**, a mild alkaloid that drives the heat and — more usefully — substantially increases the bioavailability of several other compounds, most famously curcumin from turmeric. Piperine also has modest anti-inflammatory associations in the literature in its own right. Both Longo and Blueprint use pepper as a daily seasoning, and Longo's turmeric pairings rely on it directly.

**How to consume.** Grind fresh when possible — pre-ground pepper loses its volatile aromatics within weeks of grinding. Add it toward the end of cooking; long simmering blunts the flavour and degrades piperine. For turmeric dishes, a generous twist of black pepper plus a fat (olive oil, coconut milk) is the canonical pairing for curcumin absorption.

**When to consume.** Daily, with any savoury meal. No time-of-day constraint.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — pepper is a culinary spice, not an essential nutrient. Diets without it simply lose the piperine bioavailability boost on other compounds, most notably turmeric.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Very large doses can irritate the stomach lining, particularly in people prone to reflux.

### bay-leaf

**Bay leaf** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Bay leaves are aromatic — eucalyptol, linalool, and a handful of other essential-oil compounds infuse during long simmers and lift the perceived complexity of broths, stews, and braises. The teaching value isn't about a single bioactive payload; it's that bay is one of the cheap, shelf-stable flavour tools that lets a low-salt, low-fat stew taste finished. Longo's bean stews and Blueprint's slow-cooked vegetable bases both lean on it.

**How to consume.** Add whole, dried leaves at the start of a simmer and remove before serving — the flavour develops over 30+ minutes of gentle heat and the leaf itself is too tough to chew comfortably. One or two leaves is enough for a family pot. Pair with garlic, onion, and tomato in Mediterranean cooking, or with cardamom and clove in Indian-style braises. Fresh leaves are stronger than dried — halve the count.

**When to consume.** Cooking ingredient, not a finishing spice. Used at lunch or dinner in slow-cooked dishes.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Whole leaves should be removed before serving to avoid a swallowing hazard.

### cinnamon

**Cinnamon** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Cinnamon's signature compounds are **cinnamaldehyde** (the warm aroma) and a family of polyphenols with modest post-meal-glucose-blunting effects in trial data. Two cinnamons sit on the shelf: **Ceylon ("true") cinnamon** is paler, more fragile, and milder; **Cassia** is darker, more common, and stronger-tasting. Cassia carries notably higher **coumarin**, which matters only at sustained high daily doses (a teaspoon or more, daily, over months) — for culinary use either is fine, but heavy users should prefer Ceylon. Blueprint uses cinnamon in its grain-free porridge for both flavour and the glucose-response association.

**How to consume.** Sprinkle into porridge, yoghurt, baked fruit, smoothies, or coffee. Pair with apple, pear, sweet potato, walnut. Heat doesn't destroy it. Buy whole sticks for long infusions (mulled tea, broths) and ground for everyday use.

**When to consume.** Most commonly at breakfast in both reference patterns. Daily use at culinary doses is fine.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** At culinary intake, no commonly-reported excess pattern. Daily heaped tablespoons of Cassia cinnamon over months have been associated in case reports with liver-marker changes from the coumarin load — culinary use stays well below that threshold.

### cumin

**Cumin** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Cumin's aroma comes from **cuminaldehyde** and related terpenes, with a traditional reputation in Mediterranean and South Asian cooking for supporting digestion of pulses and grains. The literature is thin on hard mechanistic claims, but cumin is one of the spices that lets bean-and-lentil-heavy cooking — the backbone of Longo's pattern — taste varied across the week.

**How to consume.** Toast whole seeds briefly in a dry pan or in oil at the start of cooking to bloom the aromatics, then grind, or use pre-ground added at the start of a sauté. Ground cumin in a dry jar fades within months; whole seeds keep their punch for a year or more. Pair with coriander seed (the canonical Indian duo), with chickpeas and lentils, or with carrot and squash.

**When to consume.** Cooking spice for lunch and dinner. No time-of-day constraint.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### coriander-seed

**Coriander (ground)** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Coriander seed (the dried fruit of the cilantro plant) carries **linalool** as its signature aroma compound, plus a small dose of polyphenols. Traditional use across Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, and Indian cuisines treats it as a digestive aid for pulse-heavy meals. The flavour is citrus-and-nutty — distinct from fresh cilantro leaf despite the shared plant.

**How to consume.** Toast whole seeds briefly before grinding for the best aroma. Ground coriander fades fast — buy small jars or grind your own. Pairs canonically with cumin (most curry pastes and many North African spice blends use the two together), and with lentils, carrots, and roasted vegetables. Useful in salad dressings for a citrus note without acid.

**When to consume.** Cooking spice for lunch and dinner. No time-of-day constraint.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### cardamom

**Cardamom** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Green cardamom pods carry **cineole** and **terpinene** as their primary aromatics — warm, floral, faintly camphorous. The traditional reputation across South Asian and Middle Eastern cooking is for digestive support and breath-freshening, and modern data points to modest antioxidant and blood-pressure associations at sustained intake. It's expensive per gram but punches above its weight in small doses.

**How to consume.** Buy whole green pods when possible — pre-ground cardamom loses its aromatics within months. Crack the pods, use whole in long simmers (broths, rice, chai), or grind the seeds for baked goods and spice blends. Pairs with cinnamon, clove, and milk-based dishes; one of the four pillars (with cumin, coriander, and clove) of garam masala.

**When to consume.** Works at any meal — breakfast porridges, savoury rice dishes, evening teas. No constraint.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### nutmeg

**Nutmeg** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Nutmeg carries **myristicin** and **elemicin** as its signature compounds, giving the warm, sweet-savoury aroma used across European baking, Italian pasta fillings, and Middle Eastern meat dishes. At culinary doses (pinches, grates of a fraction of a teaspoon) it's a flavour tool with no meaningful nutritional contribution beyond aromatic complexity. Longo's spinach-and-ricotta-style fillings use it traditionally.

**How to consume.** Buy whole nutmegs and grate fresh on a fine microplane — pre-ground nutmeg loses its character within months. A few grates is enough for a family dish. Pairs with dairy and dairy alternatives (white sauces, ricotta, milk puddings), with spinach, with squash and sweet potato, and with apple and pear in baking.

**When to consume.** Cooking ingredient, fine at any meal.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** At culinary doses (grates, pinches), no commonly-reported excess pattern. Large doses — on the order of tablespoons — are genuinely unwise: myristicin at that volume is psychoactive and can produce nausea, racing heart, and disorienting effects lasting hours. Worth knowing but trivially avoided in normal cooking.

### clove-spice

**Clove (whole)** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Cloves carry **eugenol** as their dominant compound — one of the highest eugenol concentrations of any culinary spice — which delivers the warm, numbing-sweet aroma and a strong antioxidant signature in lab assays. Used sparingly across Mediterranean, North African, and South Asian cooking. Garam masala and mulling spice blends rely on it.

**How to consume.** Use whole cloves in long simmers (stocks, mulled tea, braises, biryanis) and remove before serving, or grind a small amount for baking and spice blends. One or two cloves flavours an entire pot; ground clove is potent and easy to overdose on flavour. Pairs with cinnamon, cardamom, bay leaf, and citrus peel.

**When to consume.** Cooking ingredient. Works in evening drinks (mulled herbal tea) as well as savoury dishes.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** At culinary doses, no commonly-reported excess pattern. Concentrated clove oil and very large culinary doses of eugenol can stress the liver and irritate mucous membranes — worth knowing for anyone tempted to self-medicate with clove oil, irrelevant for normal cooking.

### turmeric

**Turmeric** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Turmeric's signature compound is **curcumin**, with one of the strongest anti-inflammatory associations in the spice literature. The catch — and it's a big one — is that **curcumin bioavailability is poor on its own**: most of an oral dose passes through unabsorbed. Two pairings change that meaningfully. **Black pepper (piperine)** raises curcumin absorption by an order of magnitude, and **fat** (olive oil, coconut milk) helps carry curcumin across the gut wall. Longo's golden-milk-style and dahl-style pairings reflect this directly; turmeric without pepper and fat is mostly colour.

**How to consume.** Add ground turmeric to oil-based sautés, coconut-milk curries, lentil dahls, or roasted vegetables — always with a generous twist of black pepper and a real fat source. Fresh turmeric root grates like ginger and brings a brighter flavour. The yellow stains everything (cutting boards, fingers, fabric) — handle with that in mind.

**When to consume.** Daily is reasonable in both reference patterns. No time-of-day constraint, though warm turmeric drinks land most naturally in the evening.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — turmeric is a culinary spice, not an essential nutrient.

**Excess signs.** At culinary doses, no commonly-reported excess pattern. High-dose turmeric supplements (multi-gram capsules) have been linked in case reports to liver-marker changes, particularly in combination with certain medications — culinary use is well below that range.

### paprika

**Paprika (incl. smoked)** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Paprika is ground dried red pepper, carrying carotenoids — particularly **capsanthin** and **beta-carotene** — that give the deep red colour and a modest antioxidant payload. Sweet paprika is mild; hot paprika brings capsaicin heat; **smoked (pimentón) paprika** adds wood-smoke aromatics from drying over oak fires and is the backbone of Spanish cuisine. Both Blueprint and Longo use paprika as a colour-and-flavour multiplier on roasted vegetables, beans, and seafood.

**How to consume.** Add early in cooking, ideally bloomed briefly in warm oil — the carotenoids are fat-soluble and the colour and aroma open up. Avoid burning it; paprika scorches fast and turns bitter. Smoked paprika pairs canonically with chickpeas, potato, eggs, and white fish. Sweet paprika dusts well over a finished dish.

**When to consume.** Cooking spice, fine at any meal.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### chili

**Chili / cayenne / peperoncino** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Chili's signature compound is **capsaicin**, which drives the heat and carries genuine associations in cohort data: modest metabolic-rate uplift, post-meal-glucose blunting, and — in large studies — lower all-cause mortality among regular chili eaters versus non-eaters. Both Longo (peperoncino) and Blueprint use chili routinely. The heat itself isn't the point; the bioactive payload is.

**How to consume.** Dried flakes, cayenne powder, or fresh chili all work — pick what suits the dish. Add early to oil-based cooking to bloom the capsaicin into the fat (it's fat-soluble), or sprinkle at the end for a sharper, more aromatic kick. Pairs with olive oil, garlic, tomato, and pulses across Mediterranean cooking. Start small and scale up; tolerance builds quickly.

**When to consume.** Daily is reasonable. Most people prefer it at lunch and dinner rather than first thing in the morning, but there's no strict rule.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Stomach irritation and reflux in sensitive eaters at high daily volumes. Otherwise no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake — even very spice-tolerant cuisines show no clear excess signal in cohort data.

### chipotle

**Chipotle (smoked)** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Chipotle is jalapeño that's been ripened red and dried over wood smoke, concentrating the capsaicin and adding the smoke aromatics on top. The capsaicin profile carries the same cohort associations as chili broadly — modest metabolic and cardiovascular signals — and the smoke compounds add a savoury depth that lets vegetarian and pescatarian cooking lean less on cured meats for that flavour register.

**How to consume.** Sold as whole dried pods, ground powder, or in adobo sauce. Powder is the easiest entry point — a quarter-teaspoon transforms a pot of beans, a roast tray of squash, or a tomato sauce. Bloom in warm oil early in cooking. Pairs with black beans, sweet potato, corn, and tomato in Mexican-style dishes; with chickpeas and eggplant outside that tradition.

**When to consume.** Cooking spice, fine at lunch or dinner.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. The capsaicin caveats from chili apply identically.

### oregano

**Oregano** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Oregano carries **carvacrol** and **thymol** as its signature compounds — both with strong antioxidant and antimicrobial profiles in lab assays. Among dried culinary herbs, oregano sits near the top of the polyphenol-per-gram ranking. It's a Mediterranean staple, used freely across Longo's tomato sauces, bean stews, and roasted-vegetable dishes.

**How to consume.** Dried oregano holds its character well — often better than dried versions of softer herbs. Add to tomato-based sauces early, sprinkle on roasted vegetables before they go in the oven, or finish a salad with it. Pairs canonically with tomato, garlic, olive oil, and feta. Greek oregano (rigani) is more pungent than Italian; Mexican oregano is botanically different and leans more citrus-and-anise.

**When to consume.** Cooking herb. Fine at any savoury meal.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### thyme-dried

**Thyme (dried)** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Thyme carries **thymol** as its dominant compound — sharing some bioactive territory with oregano — plus a complement of polyphenols. Among Mediterranean herbs it's one of the most heat-stable, holding its flavour through long roasts and braises better than basil or parsley. Both reference patterns use it across roasted vegetables, bean dishes, and fish.

**How to consume.** Dried thyme adds well at the start of cooking — long heat draws the aromatics out without destroying them. Pairs with rosemary and bay in slow-cooked dishes, with lemon and garlic on roasted chicken or fish, and with white beans and tomato. A teaspoon of dried thyme is roughly equivalent to a tablespoon of fresh.

**When to consume.** Cooking herb, fine at any meal.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### rosemary-dried

**Rosemary (dried)** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Rosemary carries **carnosic acid** and **rosmarinic acid** as its signature compounds, with strong antioxidant profiles in lab assays and a particular literature signal around protecting fats from oxidation during high-heat cooking. Practically, this means rosemary added to a roasting tray helps the oils on the tray stay more stable. Longo uses it heavily on potato, bean, and roasted-vegetable dishes; Blueprint includes it in several herb-roasted-vegetable preparations.

**How to consume.** Dried rosemary is more concentrated than fresh — use sparingly and let it bloom in warm oil before adding to the pan, or roast it with the dish from the start. The leaves stay woody; grind or chop fine if eating directly, or use whole sprigs and pull them out before serving. Pairs with potato, white beans, lemon, garlic, olive oil, and stone fruit in unexpected sweet contexts.

**When to consume.** Cooking herb, lunch and dinner.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### italian-seasoning

**Italian seasoning blend** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** A pre-mixed blend, typically dried oregano, basil, thyme, rosemary, marjoram, and sometimes sage — drawing the polyphenol payload of its constituents (see those entries individually). Convenience is the point: one jar covers most of the savoury Mediterranean herb register.

**How to consume.** Add early in cooking for tomato sauces, bean stews, and roasted vegetables; or sprinkle on flatbreads, focaccia, and salad dressings. Pairs with garlic, olive oil, and tomato as the default Italian flavour base. A teaspoon goes a long way.

**When to consume.** Cooking spice, fine at any savoury meal.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### garam-masala

**Garam masala** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** A North Indian blend, typically cumin, coriander, cardamom, clove, cinnamon, black pepper, and bay — see each constituent for its individual profile. The combined aromatic complexity is the point, and the blend pairs naturally with the lentil-and-vegetable cooking that overlaps strongly with Longo's pulse-heavy pattern.

**How to consume.** Add late in cooking — most of the volatile aromatics are fragile and long simmering blunts them. A teaspoon stirred in during the final five minutes of a dahl or curry transforms the dish. Pairs canonically with lentils, chickpeas, potato, cauliflower, and yoghurt-based finishes.

**When to consume.** Cooking spice for lunch and dinner.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### vanilla-extract

**Vanilla extract** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Vanilla's signature compound is **vanillin**, with a modest antioxidant profile. The teaching value isn't bioactive; it's that vanilla extract lets sweet preparations (porridges, yoghurts, baked fruit, plant-milk drinks) taste richer and more dessert-like without added sugar. Blueprint uses it in the grain-free porridge for exactly this reason.

**How to consume.** Real vanilla extract — alcohol-based, with vanilla beans listed first — is genuinely different from synthetic vanillin in aroma complexity. A quarter-teaspoon transforms a bowl of yoghurt or porridge. Heat-stable; works in baking and warm drinks. Store away from light.

**When to consume.** Most commonly at breakfast in both reference patterns; also evening herbal teas and milks.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### zaatar

**Za'atar** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** A Levantine blend, typically dried wild thyme (or oregano), toasted sesame seed, sumac, and salt — see thyme and oregano entries for the polyphenol base, with sumac adding its own anthocyanin payload and tart citrus note. The sesame contributes a small dose of fat and minerals. The blend slots cleanly into Mediterranean cooking despite its origin.

**How to consume.** Mix with olive oil into a paste and brush onto flatbread before baking; sprinkle on labneh, hummus, eggs, or roasted vegetables; finish a salad with it. The sesame turns rancid faster than the herbs — buy small jars and store cool.

**When to consume.** Works at any meal — breakfast on yoghurt or eggs, lunch on flatbread, dinner on roasted vegetables.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** The salt in commercial blends is the only meaningful concern at heavy daily volumes. Otherwise no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### butterfly-pea-flower

**Butterfly pea flower (powder)** — _SPICES_.

**Benefits.** Butterfly pea flower is one of the more visually striking ingredients in Blueprint's daily protocol — a deep electric blue from its **anthocyanin** pigment family (the same class that colours blueberries and red cabbage). Blueprint uses it in the grain-free porridge for the anthocyanin polyphenol contribution and the distinctive colour. The polyphenol payload is real but modest; this is not a megadose ingredient.

**How to consume.** Sold as dried whole flowers or as ground powder. The powder stirs into porridges, smoothies, and plant milks; the whole flowers steep into a bright blue tea that turns purple with lemon (pH-sensitive anthocyanins). Pairs naturally with vanilla, coconut, and other mild flavours that let the colour and faint earthy taste show through.

**When to consume.** Most naturally at breakfast — Blueprint's pattern — or as an evening caffeine-free herbal tea.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### water

**Water** — _OTHER_.

**Benefits.** The substrate everything else runs on. Hydration status affects cognition, exercise capacity, kidney function, skin appearance, and the comfort of high-fibre eating patterns like both reference diets. Neither Longo nor Blueprint treats water as an afterthought, but they emphasise it differently. Longo leans on **herbal teas and water with meals**, framing hydration as an organic part of the Mediterranean pattern rather than a metric to chase. Blueprint runs **structured intake** — measured volumes at specific points of the day, with mineral content and timing specified.

**How to consume.** Plain water is the default. Tap water is fine in most jurisdictions; a simple carbon filter handles taste and chlorine. **Herbal teas** count toward daily fluids and bring small polyphenol payloads — Longo's pattern uses them generously. **Coffee and tea** are well-tolerated in both patterns: green tea sits comfortably across the day, coffee fits in the morning half. Both contribute to total fluid intake; the diuretic effect of caffeine is modest in habitual drinkers.

**When to consume.** Across the day rather than in big boluses. With meals is fine (the old "dilutes digestion" worry has no real basis). Sip in the morning to rehydrate after the overnight fast; ease up in the final couple of hours before bed to avoid sleep interruption.

**Under-hydration patterns.** Diets and lifestyles chronically low on fluids commonly track with afternoon fatigue, headaches, constipation, darker urine, and cognitive sluggishness. The simplest self-check is urine colour: pale straw is the target, dark amber is the prompt to drink more. Dry climates, exercise, and high-fibre eating all push the requirement up.

**Over-hydration patterns.** At extremes — typically endurance athletes drinking large plain-water volumes during long events, or rare obsessive over-drinking — sodium levels can dilute to the point of hyponatremia, which presents as nausea, confusion, and at the severe end, seizure. The everyday version is far gentler: forcing several litres a day when not thirsty mostly just produces frequent bathroom trips and washes out water-soluble vitamins. Drink to thirst, with structure as a hedge if thirst signals are unreliable for you.
