### sunflower-oil

**Sunflower oil** — _OILS_VINEGARS_WINE_.

**Benefits.** A neutral-tasting plant oil pressed from sunflower seeds, dominated by linoleic acid (omega-6) in conventional varieties, with high-oleic cultivars offering a more monounsaturated profile closer to olive oil. Vitamin E content is meaningful — sunflower oil is one of the richer dietary sources of alpha-tocopherol. Both Blueprint and Longo prefer EVOO as the default culinary fat; Longo permits sunflower oil for occasional delicate frying where olive oil's flavour would dominate. It is a fat with a different profile, not a problem fat.

**How to consume.** Best reserved for situations where a neutral flavour matters — light pan-frying of fritters, batters, or fish — and for moderate-heat applications where olive oil's smoke point or taste isn't a fit. Look for high-oleic sunflower oil if available; it holds up better to heat and shifts the fatty-acid balance away from the omega-6-heavy standard pressing. Store in a dark cupboard; polyunsaturated oils oxidise faster than monounsaturated ones.

**When to consume.** Occasional use, not daily backbone. The pattern in both reference diets is to keep sunflower oil as a secondary cooking fat, with EVOO carrying the daily intake. No fasting-window concern.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category on its own. Diets chronically low in vitamin E across all sources track with neuromuscular and skin-barrier complaints in cohort data, but sunflower oil is one of many possible vehicles for that nutrient.

**Excess signs.** Diets chronically high in omega-6-dominant seed oils — when paired with low omega-3 intake — are associated in some cohort analyses with a less favourable inflammatory-marker profile. The fix is restoring omega-3 balance (fatty fish for Longo followers, flax / chia / walnut for Blueprint), not demonising the oil itself.

### vinegar-balsamic

**Balsamic vinegar** — _OILS_VINEGARS_WINE_.

**Benefits.** A reduced grape-must vinegar from Modena, naturally sweet from concentrated grape sugars and rich in polyphenols inherited from the wine grapes. The dark colour signals the same polyphenol family that makes red wine and dark berries cardio-supportive in cohort data. Longo uses balsamic across his Italian dressings and finishing drizzles; Blueprint includes it more sparingly because the residual sugar bumps the glycemic profile of an otherwise low-sugar dressing.

**How to consume.** Use raw, as a finishing acid on cooked vegetables, salads, or even strawberries. A teaspoon of aged balsamic on roasted brassicas does what a tablespoon of dressing tries to do. Cheaper "balsamic vinegar of Modena" is often standard wine vinegar with caramel colour and grape-must concentrate — read the label and prefer the unblended traditional aceto balsamico when budget allows. Don't boil it; the polyphenols and the aromatic notes degrade.

**When to consume.** Daily, in small amounts. There's no fasting-window concern at typical drizzle quantities (1–2 teaspoons).

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — vinegars are condiments, not essential nutrients.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. The residual sugar in balsamic is meaningful only if a diet stacks several sweetened condiments together.

### vinegar-red-wine

**Red wine vinegar** — _OILS_VINEGARS_WINE_.

**Benefits.** Fermented red wine yields acetic acid alongside residual polyphenols from the grape skins — a sharper, drier vinegar than balsamic with essentially no residual sugar. Acetic acid itself is associated in small clinical studies with a modest blunting of post-meal glucose response when taken with carbohydrate-rich meals. Longo uses red wine vinegar in dressings across the Italian recipes; Blueprint reaches for it whenever a low-sugar acid is needed.

**How to consume.** Standard dressing acid alongside EVOO at a roughly 1:3 vinegar-to-oil ratio. Works on bean salads, lentil dishes, and roasted vegetables where balsamic's sweetness would overpower the dish. Add at the end of cooking, not during — heat dulls the brightness.

**When to consume.** Daily, in dressing-sized portions. No fasting-window concern.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. People with reflux sometimes report that vinegar-heavy meals aggravate symptoms; that is an individual sensitivity rather than a population pattern.

### vinegar-white-wine

**White wine vinegar** — _OILS_VINEGARS_WINE_.

**Benefits.** Lighter and cleaner than red wine vinegar, with the same acetic-acid backbone and slightly fewer residual polyphenols (white grape skins contribute less colour and less anthocyanin). Used across Longo's lighter Mediterranean recipes — fish preparations, lighter bean salads, vegetable pickling. Blueprint uses it interchangeably with red wine vinegar wherever a neutral acid is wanted.

**How to consume.** Best for dishes where a darker vinegar would muddy the plate visually or flavour-wise — pale fish, white beans, cucumber salads, light vegetable pickles. Same 1:3 ratio with olive oil for dressings. Quick-pickling vegetables in diluted white wine vinegar (with salt, herbs, a touch of warmth) is a low-effort way to add a tangy element to weekday plates.

**When to consume.** Daily, in condiment quantities. No fasting-window concern.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### wine-cooking

**Cooking wine** — _OILS_VINEGARS_WINE_.

**Benefits.** A splash of dry wine — white for fish and lighter pasta sauces, red for tomato-based braises and stews — adds acidity, depth, and a small polyphenol contribution from the grapes. Most of the alcohol cooks off during simmering; what remains is the wine's aromatic and acid signature. Longo's Italian sauces use cooking wine as a deglazing agent across many of the savoury recipes; Blueprint uses it less frequently.

**How to consume.** Use wine you would actually drink — "cooking wine" sold pre-salted in supermarkets is generally worse than a cheap drinking wine. Add it after the soffrito has softened, let it reduce until the harsh alcohol smell is gone (2–3 minutes of active simmer), then continue with the rest of the recipe. Skip the salt the wine bottle's label assumes you don't have access to.

**When to consume.** Used in cooking, not consumed standalone. No fasting-window issue at the small per-serving residual.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** People who avoid alcohol entirely — for medical, religious, or recovery reasons — should know that even simmered dishes retain a small residual ethanol fraction (typically 5–15% of the added alcohol depending on simmer time). Substitute with broth and a splash of vinegar if that matters to you.

### anchovy

**Anchovy** — _SEAFOOD_.

**Benefits.** A small oily fish dense in long-chain omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA), high-quality protein, and selenium, with calcium from the soft bones in the canned form. Because anchovies sit low on the food chain and live short lives, they accumulate far less mercury than larger predatory fish. Blueprint is plant-only and excludes anchovies entirely. Longo's Longevity Diet explicitly permits 2–3 servings of small fish per week and names anchovies among the preferred choices.

**How to consume.** For Longo followers: salt-cured or oil-packed anchovies dissolve into the soffrito and add savoury depth without overt fishiness — a classic Mediterranean trick for elevating tomato sauces and braises. Rinse the salt-cured kind briefly before using. Two or three fillets per serving is plenty. For Blueprint followers: skip; the same umami can come from miso, tamari, or nutritional yeast.

**When to consume.** Within Longo's pattern, 1–2 of the weekly small-fish servings. Inside the 12h eating window with the meal they accompany.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in long-chain omega-3s — common in fully plant-based patterns without supplementation — track in cohort data with dry skin, brittle nails, and worse cardiovascular markers. Blueprint addresses this with algae-derived omega-3 supplementation; Longo addresses it directly through small fish.

**Excess signs.** Anchovies are heavily salted; a diet chronically high in salt-cured fish stacks with other sodium sources and may push blood-pressure markers upward in salt-sensitive people.

### sardine

**Sardine** — _SEAFOOD_.

**Benefits.** Another small, short-lived oily fish — sardines deliver one of the most favourable nutrient profiles in the entire seafood category: high EPA / DHA, complete protein, vitamin D, B12, selenium, and bioavailable calcium from the edible soft bones. Mercury accumulation is minimal because of their position low on the food chain. Blueprint excludes them as a plant-only protocol. Longo names sardines alongside anchovies as preferred small-fish choices, with a 2–3 servings-per-week allowance.

**How to consume.** Fresh sardines grilled whole over high heat with lemon and olive oil are a Mediterranean staple. Canned sardines (in olive oil or water, not soybean oil) are one of the highest-nutrient-per-euro foods in any supermarket — eat them with the bones for the calcium. Pair with bitter greens, whole-grain bread, or a tomato salad. A squeeze of lemon helps with the iron absorption.

**When to consume.** Within Longo's pattern, 1–2 of the weekly small-fish servings, inside the 12h eating window. There's no time-of-day rule beyond that.

**Deficiency signs.** Same pattern as anchovies — diets chronically low in long-chain omega-3s correlate with dry skin, hair, and worse lipid markers in cohort data. Sardines also provide vitamin D, which is harder to source from plant foods.

**Excess signs.** Canned sardines are salted; in salt-sensitive people, frequent canned-fish meals can contribute to blood-pressure pressure. No commonly-reported excess pattern at 2–3 servings a week.

### octopus

**Octopus** — _SEAFOOD_.

**Benefits.** A cephalopod whose muscle is notably high in protein and low in fat compared with finfish, with meaningful selenium, B12, copper, and iron. Cooked octopus is a Mediterranean coastal staple from Spain to Greece. Blueprint excludes it as a plant-only protocol. Longo's Longevity Diet permits seafood within its 2–3 servings-per-week guidance; octopus is acceptable though not specifically singled out the way sardines and anchovies are.

**How to consume.** Octopus needs either long, gentle simmering (45–60 minutes) or quick high-heat charring after a tenderising step, depending on the cut and tradition. The classic Galician preparation — boiled and dressed with olive oil, paprika, and coarse salt — keeps the fat backbone Mediterranean. Avoid deep-frying batters that add a heavy refined-oil load.

**When to consume.** Within Longo's pattern, as one of the weekly seafood servings if you enjoy it. No fasting-window concern beyond fitting inside the 12h eating window.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — see the broader low-omega-3 pattern under sardines and anchovies.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Octopus's purine content is moderate; people managing gout with their clinician sometimes track purine-heavy foods more carefully.

### squid-cuttlefish

**Squid / cuttlefish** — _SEAFOOD_.

**Benefits.** Cephalopod muscle like octopus — high protein, low fat, with copper, selenium, B12, and a small dose of long-chain omega-3s. The cooking properties differ from finfish: squid and cuttlefish toughen if cooked between roughly 1 and 20 minutes, requiring either very quick high-heat cooking or long gentle braising. Blueprint excludes as plant-only. Longo's Longevity Diet permits within the 2–3 weekly seafood servings.

**How to consume.** Either flash-grill or flash-sear rings and tentacles over very high heat for under two minutes, or braise whole bodies in a tomato-and-wine sauce for 30–45 minutes until tender. Stuffed cuttlefish with breadcrumbs, parsley, and garlic is a Mediterranean coastal classic. Squid ink is edible and adds umami and colour to pasta.

**When to consume.** As one of the weekly seafood servings within Longo's pattern. Inside the 12h eating window.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Cephalopods carry moderate cholesterol; in older cohort studies this raised concerns, but current evidence does not link dietary cholesterol from whole-food seafood to cardiovascular outcomes the way refined-fat and refined-carbohydrate intakes do. No commonly-reported excess pattern at 1–2 servings a week.

### snapper

**Snapper** — _SEAFOOD_.

**Benefits.** A lean white-fleshed finfish (red snapper most commonly) with high-quality protein, selenium, B12, and a modest amount of omega-3 — less than oily fish like sardines, but the protein density and the low-fat profile fit a longevity pattern well. Blueprint excludes as plant-only. Longo's Longevity Diet permits within the 2–3 weekly seafood servings; lean whitefish counts toward that allowance.

**How to consume.** Bake whole with herbs and lemon, or fillet and pan-sear skin-side down in olive oil. Snapper takes well to Mediterranean preparations — tomato, capers, olives, white wine. Avoid overcooking; flesh should just flake. A whole baked fish on a sheet pan over vegetables is a low-effort weeknight option that lands inside Longo's framework.

**When to consume.** As one of the weekly seafood servings within Longo's pattern, inside the 12h eating window.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. The broader low-omega-3 pattern (see sardines) applies if seafood is absent and not substituted.

**Excess signs.** Snapper is mid-tier on the food chain — mercury accumulation is moderate, lower than tuna or swordfish but higher than sardines or anchovies. Diets that lean heavily on large predatory fish over years can accumulate mercury; rotating among small oily fish and occasional whitefish is the standard mitigation.

### fish-stew-mix

**Mixed fish (for stew)** — _SEAFOOD_.

**Benefits.** Stew mixes typically combine firm whitefish, mussels or clams, sometimes shrimp, and occasionally a piece of oily fish — the variety delivers complete protein, B12, selenium, iodine, and a moderate omega-3 contribution depending on the mix. Mediterranean fish stews (cacciucco, bouillabaisse, suquet) are a traditional pattern Longo's Longevity Diet accommodates. Blueprint excludes as plant-only.

**How to consume.** Build a base of soffrito (olive oil, onion, garlic, tomato, herbs), add the firmer fish first, then mussels and shellfish that cook quickly, finishing with herbs and a squeeze of lemon. A slice of toasted whole-grain bread under the broth catches the saffron-and-olive-oil base. Buy whatever the local fishmonger has fresh that day rather than a frozen pre-mix; freshness matters more than the specific species.

**When to consume.** As one of the weekly seafood servings within Longo's pattern. A stew counts as one serving even if it contains multiple species.

**Deficiency signs.** See the broader low-omega-3 pattern under sardines.

**Excess signs.** Stews lean on salty broth and shellfish, both of which carry sodium. No commonly-reported excess pattern at a serving or two a week; salt-sensitive people watch the broth volume.

### olive-table

**Table olive** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** Whole cured olives carry the same monounsaturated-fat backbone as olive oil — oleic acid plus polyphenols (hydroxytyrosol, oleuropein) — with the added fibre of the whole fruit. The curing process (brine, dry-salt, lye, or oil) preserves much of the polyphenol load. Longo's Mediterranean pattern features olives as a regular snack and pasta-sauce ingredient; Blueprint includes them in some savoury recipes for the same fat and polyphenol payload.

**How to consume.** As a snack alongside whole-grain bread, in puttanesca-style pasta sauces with capers and tomato, on salads, or chopped into tapenades. A handful (roughly 8–10 olives) per snack portion is a useful anchor. Prefer olives cured traditionally — water-or-brine-cured varieties retain more polyphenols than lye-treated commodity black olives. Don't discard the residual oil in the jar if it's good olive oil; use it in a dressing.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine. No fasting-window concern; olives sit inside the eating window with the meal or snack they accompany.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category on its own.

**Excess signs.** Olives are salted heavily during curing — a diet that stacks olives with anchovies, capers, and cured cheeses can push sodium intake high enough to matter for blood-pressure markers in salt-sensitive people. Rinse heavily-brined olives briefly if sodium is a concern.

### caper

**Caper** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** The brined flower bud of the caper bush carries quercetin and rutin — two flavonoids with antioxidant activity in laboratory and small clinical studies — plus the briny acid that lifts Mediterranean fish and pasta dishes. The bioactive contribution per serving is small because portions are small, but capers join the broader polyphenol-rich Mediterranean fingerprint. Longo uses them across the puttanesca-family sauces; Blueprint uses them more sparingly.

**How to consume.** Rinse salt-packed capers before using to control the sodium; brine-packed capers are milder and can go straight in. Pair with olives, tomato, anchovies, and parsley for the classic Italian flavour profile. Add toward the end of cooking — long simmering dulls their bright tang.

**When to consume.** As a condiment, daily is fine. No fasting-window concern at culinary doses.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Sodium is the main consideration. No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### chocolate-dark

**Dark chocolate / cacao** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** Cacao is one of the densest dietary sources of flavanols — particularly epicatechin, associated with improved endothelial function in clinical studies. The polyphenol load drops sharply as cacao percentage drops and as added sugar climbs. Blueprint uses cacao or dark chocolate (80%+) in dessert recipes precisely for this flavanol payload; Longo's Mediterranean pattern accommodates small daily portions of dark chocolate as the traditional Italian sweet finish.

**How to consume.** Aim for chocolate that is at least 80% cacao — higher if your palate accepts it — and treat it as a savouring portion (15–25 g, two or three small squares). Bitter, unsweetened cacao nibs offer a similar flavanol profile and can be sprinkled on yogurt, oats, or smoothies. Dutched (alkalised) cocoa loses a meaningful fraction of its flavanols; non-alkalised cocoa is the higher-polyphenol form.

**When to consume.** Daily, in small portions, inside the eating window. Some people find that chocolate close to bedtime disrupts sleep — the theobromine and small caffeine load are mild stimulants for sensitive individuals.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Most "dark chocolate" in supermarkets is 50–70% cacao with significant added sugar; a daily 100 g bar in that range carries the same metabolic load as any sweetened dessert. The flavanol benefit doesn't outrun a sugar excess.

### mustard-dijon

**Dijon mustard** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** Mustard is a brassica condiment — ground mustard seed with vinegar, salt, and white wine in the Dijon style. It carries trace glucosinolates from the seed and adds piquancy without significant sugar (unlike most ketchups and barbecue sauces). Both Blueprint and Longo treat it as a flavour adjunct rather than a primary ingredient, useful for emulsifying vinaigrettes and lifting bean or fish dishes.

**How to consume.** A teaspoon of Dijon in a vinaigrette helps the oil and vinegar emulsify and adds a savoury base note. Whisk into bean salads, brush onto roasted vegetables, fold into yogurt-based dips. Most supermarket Dijon is fine; check the label for added sugar (good Dijon needs none).

**When to consume.** As a condiment, daily is fine. No fasting-window concern.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Sodium content is moderate; no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### tamari

**Tamari / soy sauce** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** A fermented-soy condiment that delivers concentrated umami through naturally-occurring glutamates, with traces of B vitamins from the fermentation. Tamari is the wheat-free variant of soy sauce (useful for gluten-sensitive cooks); shoyu contains wheat. Both Blueprint and Longo use small amounts as an umami booster, particularly in plant-based dishes where the savoury depth that meat or fish would otherwise provide is missing.

**How to consume.** Use in small splashes — a teaspoon or two per dish is usually enough — in stir-fries, marinades, dressings, and finishing sauces. Pair with ginger, garlic, sesame, and rice vinegar for the East Asian flavour profile. Naturally-brewed tamari (long fermentation) carries more depth than hydrolysed soy sauce substitutes.

**When to consume.** As a condiment, daily is fine. No fasting-window concern at culinary doses.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Tamari is concentrated sodium — a tablespoon delivers a meaningful fraction of a salt-conscious daily intake. Diets that lean heavily on soy-sauce-based dishes alongside other salted foods (cured fish, olives, capers) can stack high enough to matter for blood-pressure markers in salt-sensitive people.

### honey

**Honey** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** A traditional sweetener with trace polyphenols, small amounts of enzymes from the bee, and a flavour signature that varies by floral source. From a metabolic standpoint, honey is an added sugar — its fructose and glucose deliver the same glycemic and insulin response as table sugar at comparable doses. The micronutrient contribution is real but small relative to the sugar load. Both Blueprint and Longo minimise added sugars; honey is treated as the least-bad sweetener when one is needed, not as a health food.

**How to consume.** When you reach for a sweetener, raw, unfiltered honey from a known source preserves the most of the trace polyphenols and the floral character. A teaspoon to sweeten yogurt, drizzle on whole-grain toast, or finish a vinaigrette is plenty. Heating honey above ~40 °C degrades its enzymes; for cooked applications, the polyphenol angle largely disappears and honey becomes a flavour choice.

**When to consume.** Sparingly, inside the eating window. Both reference diets place sugars later in the day for some followers (after the main protein and fibre intake has blunted the glycemic response), though this is a refinement rather than a rule.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — humans do not require added sugars.

**Excess signs.** Diets chronically high in added sugars — honey included — correlate in cohort data with worse insulin-sensitivity markers, dental caries, and visceral-fat accumulation. The biological pathway is the sugar load itself, not the source.

### maple-syrup

**Maple syrup** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** The boiled-down sap of sugar maples, predominantly sucrose with trace manganese, zinc, and small amounts of polyphenols specific to the maple. Dark, late-season grades carry slightly more of these trace compounds than light grades. As with honey, the metabolic story is the sugar load: maple syrup is an added sugar that delivers the same glycemic response as cane sugar at comparable doses. Blueprint and Longo both minimise added sugars; maple syrup is a flavour-and-tradition choice, not a longevity input.

**How to consume.** As a sweetener for oats, yogurt, or whole-grain pancakes, in teaspoon quantities rather than the puddle the bottle invites. Grade A dark is the standard kitchen choice; the small premium for genuine maple syrup over corn-syrup imitations is worth paying for the flavour and the absence of high-fructose corn syrup.

**When to consume.** Sparingly, inside the eating window. Same caveats as honey about sweeteners later in the day rather than first thing on an empty stomach.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Same pattern as honey — diets chronically high in added sugars from any source track with worse insulin-sensitivity markers and visceral-fat accumulation in cohort data.

### sugar-cane

**Cane / refined sugar** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** Refined sucrose from sugarcane or sugar beet. No meaningful micronutrient contribution — the refining process strips the molasses fraction that carried what little mineral content the original cane had. From a metabolic standpoint, this is the reference added sugar against which honey and maple are compared. Both Blueprint and Longo minimise added sugars; cane sugar is the most directly minimised because it offers no flavour or polyphenol justification.

**How to consume.** Where a recipe absolutely needs it, use the smallest functional amount. Unrefined options — muscovado, panela, demerara — retain some of the molasses and a small mineral and polyphenol fraction, and have a more interesting flavour, but the metabolic story is the same as white sugar at comparable doses.

**When to consume.** Sparingly, inside the eating window. The general advice across both reference diets is to displace added sugar with fruit, dates, or small amounts of honey or maple when sweetness is wanted.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** The clearest excess pattern across the entire pedagogy: diets chronically high in refined sugar correlate in cohort data with insulin resistance, dental caries, fatty-liver markers, and visceral-fat accumulation. Reducing added sugar is one of the most consistent recommendations across longevity research, including both Blueprint and Longo.

### nutritional-yeast

**Nutritional yeast** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** Deactivated yeast flakes that deliver concentrated B vitamins — especially fortified B12, which is otherwise hard to source from plant foods — alongside a cheesy, umami flavour. Complete protein, fibre, and beta-glucans round out the profile. Blueprint uses nutritional yeast across the plant-only recipes as a cheese-replacement and B-vitamin vehicle; for anyone following a fully plant-based pattern, fortified nutritional yeast is one of the most efficient ways to keep B12 status reliable. Longo's pescatarian pattern gets B12 from fish, so the role is less critical there.

**How to consume.** Sprinkle on pasta, popcorn, roasted vegetables, soups, and salads. Stir into cashew-based "cheese" sauces or sprinkle directly. Two tablespoons in a day is a useful working portion. Look for fortified versions specifically — not all nutritional yeast carries added B12, and the fortification is what makes it valuable for plant-only patterns.

**When to consume.** Daily for plant-only patterns. No fasting-window concern.

**Deficiency signs.** B12 deficiency builds slowly over years in plant-only diets without supplementation or fortified foods — patterns include fatigue, tingling extremities, mood changes, and macrocytic-anaemia markers on blood work. Nutritional yeast is one of the cleanest dietary fixes; B12 supplementation is the other standard answer.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. People with yeast sensitivities or specific GI conditions sometimes find nutritional yeast aggravating; that is an individual sensitivity rather than a population pattern.

### tahini

**Tahini (sesame paste)** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** Ground sesame seed delivers a calcium-dense plant fat, with sesamin and sesamolin lignans that carry antioxidant activity in laboratory and small clinical studies. Tahini is one of the few plant foods that contributes meaningful calcium without dairy. Both Blueprint and Longo use it across hummus, dressings, and Middle Eastern preparations — the fat backbone is mostly unsaturated, and the protein density is useful for plant-only meals.

**How to consume.** Stir well before each use — natural tahini separates. Use as the base for hummus, baba ganoush, and lemon-tahini dressings; drizzle on roasted vegetables, grain bowls, or fish. A tablespoon thinned with lemon juice, garlic, and water makes a dressing that lifts almost any salad. Hulled (light) tahini has a milder flavour; unhulled (darker) tahini retains more of the calcium-rich seed coat.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine. Sits inside the eating window with the meal it accompanies; no fasting-window concern with fats.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category on its own. Diets chronically low in plant-based calcium sources — for people who avoid dairy and don't eat fish with bones — can drift toward low calcium intake; tahini, leafy greens, and fortified plant milks all help close the gap.

**Excess signs.** Tahini is energy-dense, comparable to other nut and seed butters. No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### liquid-smoke

**Liquid smoke** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** A water-based condiment made by condensing hardwood smoke and filtering out the heavier polycyclic aromatic compounds, leaving behind the volatile flavour molecules. A flavour adjunct, not a nutritional contributor — Blueprint uses small amounts in plant-based barbecue-flavoured dishes to mimic the smoke note without grilling. Longo's Italian Mediterranean pattern uses it rarely.

**How to consume.** A few drops go a long way. Stir into marinades, bean dishes, or tofu and tempeh preparations to add a smoky depth. Choose brands with no added sugar or caramel colour; the simpler the ingredient list the better.

**When to consume.** As a flavour condiment, no time-of-day rule.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### umami-seasoning

**Umami seasoning** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** A category covering mushroom-based powders, glutamate-rich blends, and dried-seaweed seasonings — all aimed at delivering the savoury fifth-taste depth without relying on meat or fish. Useful in plant-based cooking where the umami contribution of long-simmered animal stocks is missing. Blueprint uses umami seasonings across the plant-only savoury recipes; Longo's Italian pattern leans more on anchovies, parmesan, and tomato concentrate for the same effect.

**How to consume.** A small sprinkle in soups, stews, marinades, and grain dishes. Mushroom-powder blends are particularly effective in mushroom-forward dishes; seaweed-based blends (dulse, kombu) work well in legume soups. Read the label — some commercial "umami" mixes carry significant added sodium or MSG, which isn't a problem in itself but stacks with other sodium sources.

**When to consume.** As a flavour condiment, no time-of-day rule.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Sodium is the main consideration — most blends are salt-heavy. No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### ketchup-unsweetened

**Ketchup (unsweetened)** — _PANTRY_SWEETS_.

**Benefits.** A tomato-based condiment without the added sugar that defines standard ketchup — the lycopene contribution from concentrated tomato is preserved, and the metabolic burden of the sugared version is removed. Standard supermarket ketchup is roughly 20% sugar by weight; unsweetened versions rely on tomato sweetness, sometimes a touch of date or apple, and vinegar. Both Blueprint and Longo prefer the unsweetened variant when a ketchup-style condiment is wanted.

**How to consume.** Use as you would standard ketchup — on baked sweet-potato fries, in burger sauces, mixed into bean stews. Read labels carefully; "no sugar added" sometimes means concentrated fruit juice or natural sweeteners that carry the same metabolic load. The cleanest versions list tomato, vinegar, salt, and spices.

**When to consume.** As a condiment, no time-of-day rule.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Sodium content varies by brand. No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.
