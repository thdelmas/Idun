### celery

**Celery** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Celery is roughly 95% water with a small but useful payload of apigenin (a flavonoid implicated in anti-inflammatory pathways), vitamin K, and potassium. Its real nutritional role in both Blueprint and Longo's recipes is as a mirepoix / soffrito aromatic — celery, carrot, and onion are the base of most of Longo's vegetable soups and stews. The fibre is mostly insoluble, which supports stool bulk and transit. Calorically negligible, which makes it a free flavour-carrier in calorie-aware meal patterns.

**How to consume.** Use the stalk diced fine for soffrito; the leaves are aromatic and worth saving for broths rather than discarded. Raw with a smear of nut butter or hummus is a Blueprint-style snack. Light braising in olive oil mellows the bitter edge. Store with the cut end in water in the fridge to keep it crisp for a week.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine — celery is a background vegetable that lifts everything else without competing. It sits comfortably inside Longo's 12h eating window in soups and stews, and pairs well with the late-afternoon raw-vegetable habit common in the Mediterranean pattern.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category on its own. Diets chronically low in low-density vegetables broadly track in cohort data with lower fibre intake and lower potassium-to-sodium ratios, which the literature associates with blood-pressure pressure over years.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Very heavy raw celery juicing trends online occasionally produce GI complaints, but whole-stalk culinary use carries no meaningful excess concern.

### potato

**Potato** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Potatoes are dense in potassium (more per gram than banana), vitamin C, and B6, with resistant starch that increases when cooked then cooled. The skin carries most of the fibre and polyphenols. Longo treats potatoes as a legitimate longevity-pattern starch when cooked simply and eaten with olive oil and herbs; Blueprint leans more on sweet potato but does not exclude white potato. Glycaemic impact depends heavily on preparation — boiled-then-cooled potato salad behaves very differently from a hot baked potato.

**How to consume.** Boil, steam, or roast with skin on. Cooking and then cooling (potato salad, next-day reheats) raises resistant starch and lowers glycaemic impact. Pair with olive oil, vinegar, and herbs rather than butter and sour cream. Avoid deep-frying — industrial frying oils and the high temperatures drive the dish out of either reference pattern.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window, generally as part of a meal that includes fat and protein to flatten the glucose curve. Both Blueprint and Longo treat starches as meal-anchors, not standalone snacks.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Diets chronically low in potassium-bearing starches and high in refined-grain starches commonly track with lower satiety per calorie and harder-to-manage blood-pressure markers in long observation.

**Excess signs.** Daily large servings of fried or buttered potato — chips, fries, gratin — track in cohort studies with weight gain and worse cardiometabolic markers; the issue is the preparation and the fat quality far more than the tuber itself.

### butternut-squash

**Butternut squash** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Butternut is heavy in beta-carotene (the orange flesh tells you so), vitamin C, potassium, and a slow-digesting starch / fibre mix. Beta-carotene is a vitamin A precursor and one of the carotenoid family that cohort data associate with healthy ageing of skin and eyes. Longo's autumn soups lean on butternut as a creamy base without dairy. It carries enough sweetness to satisfy a dessert craving when roasted, which is useful for adherence to a low-added-sugar pattern.

**How to consume.** Roast in cubes with olive oil and rosemary, or simmer into a soup with a soffrito base and finish with a swirl of EVOO. The skin softens when roasted and is edible. Pair with a fat (olive oil, nuts) — beta-carotene is fat-soluble and absorbed far better with fat in the same meal. Frozen pre-cubed butternut is a reasonable shortcut.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window. Its slow starch makes it a good autumn / winter dinner anchor in Longo's 12h pattern. Blueprint uses it as a lunchtime starch alongside legumes.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in orange / yellow produce — squash, sweet potato, carrot — commonly correlate with lower carotenoid blood levels, which observational data associate with drier skin and slower eye adaptation in low light.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Very heavy daily intake can tint palms orange (carotenodermia), which is harmless and reverses on its own.

### acorn-squash

**Acorn squash** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Acorn is a milder, less sweet cousin of butternut, with a similar carotenoid and potassium profile but slightly more fibre per gram and less sugar. Vitamin C is meaningful, especially before long storage. Its bowl-shaped halves make it the easiest squash to stuff, which is how Longo's Mediterranean tradition handles it — halved, roasted, then filled with grain and herb stuffings. Closer to a vegetable in mouthfeel than butternut, which reads as a starch.

**How to consume.** Halve, scoop seeds, brush the cut face with olive oil, and roast cut-side-down until tender. Eat the flesh straight from the shell or stuff with farro, herbs, and pine nuts. The skin is technically edible but tougher than butternut's; most people skip it. Seeds can be roasted and salted like pumpkin seeds.

**When to consume.** Autumn and winter, inside the eating window. Works as either a side or the centrepiece of a vegetable-forward Longo-style dinner.

**Deficiency signs.** Same pattern as the wider orange-squash group — diets chronically low in carotenoid-rich produce track with lower blood carotenoid levels and the skin / vision patterns mentioned under butternut.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### spaghetti-squash

**Spaghetti squash** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Spaghetti squash is the odd one out in the squash cluster — meaningfully lower in carbohydrate and calories than butternut, acorn, or pumpkin, with flesh that pulls apart into pasta-like strands when cooked. Beta-carotene content is lower than the orange squashes (the flesh is pale yellow), but it carries useful potassium, vitamin C, and fibre. Its role in both Blueprint and Longo's adaptations is as a pasta-replacer for people lowering grain intake without giving up the noodle format. Adherence-friendly more than nutritionally remarkable.

**How to consume.** Halve, roast cut-side-down at 200 °C until a fork pulls strands cleanly, then scrape into a bowl and dress as you would pasta — olive oil, garlic, a tomato sauce, herbs, parmesan. Microwave works in a pinch. Don't overcook or the strands turn to mush.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window, as the base of a sauced dish. Works well for people on Longo's pattern who want pasta-mouthfeel without the grain load on a given day.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own deficiency category. The wider "diet low in vegetable volume" pattern applies.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### pumpkin

**Pumpkin** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Nutritionally close to butternut — heavy beta-carotene, potassium, vitamin C, slow-digesting starch — and used interchangeably in most longevity-leaning recipes. Pumpkin seeds (pepitas) are a separate food and a useful source of zinc and magnesium. Longo uses pumpkin in autumn soups and risottos; Blueprint treats it as one of the orange-starch options alongside sweet potato and butternut. Cohort data on carotenoid-rich produce intake support the broader association with healthy skin and eye ageing.

**How to consume.** Roast, soup, or purée into risotto. Pair with a fat — olive oil, pumpkin-seed oil, or a handful of toasted nuts — to lift carotenoid absorption. Canned 100% pumpkin purée (no added sugar) is a reasonable pantry shortcut for soups and baked goods. Save the seeds, rinse, and roast with salt.

**When to consume.** Autumn and winter, inside the eating window. Works as a dinner anchor in Longo's pattern and as a lunch starch in Blueprint's.

**Deficiency signs.** Same orange-produce pattern — diets chronically low in carotenoid-rich vegetables correlate in cohort data with lower blood carotenoid levels and the associated skin / vision markers.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Pumpkin-spice lattes are a sugar-and-cream problem, not a pumpkin problem.

### tomato

**Tomato** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Tomato's signature compound is **lycopene**, the carotenoid that gives ripe tomatoes their red colour and that cohort data tie to lower prostate-cancer incidence and to general cardiovascular benefit. Crucially, **cooking plus fat dramatically raises lycopene bioavailability** — heat breaks down cell walls and converts lycopene to its more absorbable cis form, and fat carries it into circulation. This is the mechanistic reason Longo's tradition leans so hard on olive-oil-cooked tomato sauces rather than raw tomato salad as the longevity vehicle. Tomato also supplies vitamin C, potassium, and small amounts of folate.

**How to consume.** Raw is fine and refreshing; cooked-in-olive-oil is where the lycopene work happens. A long-simmered tomato sauce, a pan of roasted cherry tomatoes finished with EVOO, or a tin of San Marzanos reduced with garlic — all deliver more lycopene than the equivalent weight of raw tomato salad. Canned and tomato-paste versions concentrate lycopene further and are pantry staples for both reference patterns. Pair with a fat.

**When to consume.** Daily inside the eating window. Tomato sauce is the connective tissue of Longo's weekday dinners; Blueprint uses tomato in soups and stews across the week.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in lycopene-bearing foods (tomato, watermelon, pink grapefruit) track in cohort data with lower blood lycopene and with the cardiovascular markers the literature ties to it.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. People with reflux sometimes find acidic tomato dishes a trigger; that is a tolerance issue, not a population pattern.

### bell-pepper

**Bell pepper** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Bell peppers are remarkably vitamin-C-dense — a single red bell pepper carries more vitamin C than an orange — and the red ones additionally bring carotenoids (beta-carotene, lutein, zeaxanthin) and capsanthin. Lutein and zeaxanthin are the macular carotenoids tied to long-term eye health. The green pepper is the unripe form and lower in carotenoids and sweetness; red, yellow, and orange are ripened and nutritionally richer. Used in both Blueprint and Longo recipes as a colour-and-crunch produce element.

**How to consume.** Raw in salads, roasted whole and peeled (the Mediterranean technique that yields silky strips for antipasti), or sautéed into a pepperonata with onion and tomato. Roasting concentrates flavour and slightly lowers vitamin C. Pair the red varieties with a fat to carry the carotenoids.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine, inside the eating window. Raw peppers as an afternoon snack are a low-friction way to lift vitamin C intake without supplementation.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in vitamin-C-rich produce track in long observation with slower wound healing, easier bruising, and the gum / dental patterns historically associated with scurvy at the extreme end.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. People sensitive to nightshades sometimes report joint discomfort patterns, but the population-level evidence for nightshade sensitivity is thin.

### eggplant

**Eggplant / aubergine** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Eggplant is mostly water and fibre with a useful payload of nasunin (the dark-purple anthocyanin in the skin) and chlorogenic acid. Nasunin is studied for its free-radical-scavenging behaviour. Calorically light, satisfying when cooked properly, and a meat-replacer in texture for vegetable-forward Mediterranean dishes — Longo's tradition uses eggplant in melanzane alla parmigiana, caponata, and in pasta sauces. Leave the skin on; that's where the colour and anthocyanin live.

**How to consume.** Eggplant absorbs oil like a sponge if you let it. Salt sliced eggplant for 30 minutes to draw out moisture and bitterness, then pat dry before roasting or grilling — this also limits oil uptake. Roast, grill, or braise rather than deep-fry. Pair with tomato, olive oil, garlic, and basil for the classic Mediterranean treatment.

**When to consume.** Late summer and autumn, inside the eating window. Works as a dinner centrepiece in Longo's pattern, especially when stuffed or layered.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own deficiency category. The broader anthocyanin family (also in berries, red cabbage, black rice) is where the cohort signal sits.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Raw or under-cooked eggplant can taste unpleasantly astringent — that is a palatability issue, not a safety one at culinary doses.

### zucchini

**Zucchini / courgette** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Zucchini is roughly 94% water, with modest vitamin C, vitamin K, and small amounts of carotenoids in the skin. Calorically negligible, which makes it a useful volume-builder in calorie-aware patterns. Its main contribution to both Blueprint and Longo recipes is texture and bulk — spiralised as a pasta-replacer, diced into ratatouille, or grilled into antipasti. Not a nutrition powerhouse on its own; the value is in what it crowds out (refined starches) when used as a vehicle.

**How to consume.** Grill, roast, or sauté with olive oil and garlic. Avoid boiling — it turns watery and loses what little flavour it has. Spiralised "zoodles" work as a pasta substitute when raw or very lightly warmed. The flower (zucchini blossom) is a delicacy in Longo's Mediterranean tradition, lightly fried or stuffed.

**When to consume.** Late spring through autumn, inside the eating window. Works as a side or as the volume base of a vegetable-forward meal.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own deficiency category. Falls under the wider "low vegetable volume" pattern.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### cucumber

**Cucumber** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Cucumber is around 96% water — the most hydrating common vegetable — with small amounts of vitamin K, potassium, and silica (concentrated in the skin). Its function in both reference patterns is hydration and crunch, not micronutrient delivery. The skin and seeds carry most of what little nutrition it has, so peeling discards the better half. Blueprint and Longo recipes use cucumber in raw salads, tzatziki-style yoghurt sides, and as a snack vehicle for hummus.

**How to consume.** Raw, with skin on, ideally unwaxed or scrubbed if waxed. Slice into salads with tomato, olive oil, and herbs (the Mediterranean default), or batonned for dipping. Quick-pickling in vinegar is a fine treatment that adds gut-friendly acidity. Keep refrigerated — cucumbers wilt quickly at room temperature.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine, inside the eating window. Useful in summer for hydration and as a low-friction raw-vegetable habit.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own category. Diets chronically low in water-dense raw produce can show up as lower urinary volume and harder stool patterns over time, especially in hot climates.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### spinach

**Spinach** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Spinach is one of the most micronutrient-dense leaves on the planet — folate, vitamin K, iron, magnesium, lutein, zeaxanthin, and nitrates in meaningful quantities. The nitrate content is the vasodilation mechanism cohort data tie to lower blood pressure on green-leafy-heavy diets. Blueprint's "Nutty Pudding" tradition and the daily green-leaf habit make spinach (or equivalents) a near-daily staple. Longo's tradition uses spinach in soups, pasta fillings, and sautéed sides. Iron is non-haem and absorbed far better with vitamin C in the same meal.

**How to consume.** Raw in salads, wilted briefly with olive oil and garlic, or blended into smoothies. Cooking shrinks volume dramatically — a large pan of raw spinach yields a small portion cooked. Pair with a vitamin-C source (lemon, tomato, pepper) to lift iron absorption. The oxalate content is real; if you have a kidney-stone history, rotate spinach with lower-oxalate leaves (chard, lettuce) rather than eating it daily.

**When to consume.** Daily inside the eating window. Both reference patterns treat green leaves as a daily, not weekly, habit.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in green leaves track in long observation with lower folate, lower vitamin K, lower dietary nitrate, and the cardiovascular markers tied to all three.

**Excess signs.** Very heavy daily raw-spinach intake (smoothie-volume) can contribute to oxalate load in people predisposed to kidney stones. At normal culinary intake, no commonly-reported excess pattern.

### nettle

**Nettle** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Stinging nettle is a wild green with an unusually high mineral content for a leaf — iron, calcium, magnesium, vitamin K — plus a useful protein fraction by dry weight. Traditional in Northern Italian, French, and British country cooking, and a recurring spring green in Longo's regional tradition (nettle risotto, nettle gnudi, nettle soup). The sting (formic acid in the trichomes) is fully neutralised by heat or by drying.

**How to consume.** Harvest young spring tops with gloves, blanch 30 seconds in boiling water (this kills the sting), then drain and use exactly as you would spinach — in soups, risottos, fillings, or sautéed with olive oil and garlic. Dried nettle makes a mineral-rich tea. Older summer plants get stringy and develop cystoliths; stick to young tops.

**When to consume.** Spring, inside the eating window. A seasonal green that shouldn't be a daily year-round food — it is more powerful as a foraged spring habit than as a supermarket staple.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own category. Diets chronically low in wild / bitter greens miss out on the broader mineral and phytochemical diversity that cohort data on foraged-food traditions associate with healthy ageing.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. The sting is the only safety concern, and it disappears with cooking.

### escarole-endive

**Escarole / endive** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Escarole and Belgian endive are bitter chicory-family leaves rich in inulin (a prebiotic fibre that feeds beneficial gut bacteria), folate, vitamin K, and intybin (the bitter compound that stimulates bile flow and supports fat digestion). Longo's tradition leans heavily on bitter chicories — escarole and beans is a Neapolitan staple, and endive appears in salads year-round. The bitterness itself is the point: bitter receptors on the tongue trigger the upstream digestive cascade that sweet-only palates miss.

**How to consume.** Raw in salads with a sharp vinaigrette, braised slowly with olive oil and garlic until the bitterness mellows, or wilted into a bean soup. Pair with a fat and an acid — the bitterness wants to be balanced, not erased. Belgian endive grills beautifully cut into halves.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window, ideally with or before a meal — the bitter signal primes digestion.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in bitter leaves and prebiotic fibre commonly track in cohort data with less microbial diversity in the gut and with the digestive complaint patterns (bloating, sluggish transit) tied to low-fibre diets generally.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### radicchio

**Radicchio** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Radicchio is the deep-red Italian chicory and shares escarole's bitter-leaf benefits — inulin, intybin, folate, vitamin K — with the additional anthocyanin payload that gives it its colour. Anthocyanins are the same polyphenol family that drives the cardiovascular signal in berries and red wine. Longo's Veneto and Friuli traditions treat radicchio as a winter staple — Treviso radicchio grilled with olive oil, Castelfranco radicchio in salads. The bitterness mellows substantially with heat.

**How to consume.** Raw shredded into salads with citrus and walnut, grilled in wedges with olive oil and balsamic, or wilted into a risotto (risotto al radicchio is the canonical Veneto dish). Pair with something sweet or fatty to balance — pear, walnut, parmesan, balsamic reduction all work.

**When to consume.** Autumn and winter, inside the eating window. A seasonal bitter green that earns its place on the weekly rotation when escarole and endive get monotonous.

**Deficiency signs.** Same bitter-leaf pattern as escarole — diets chronically low in bitter chicories miss the inulin / intybin payload and commonly correlate with the gut-diversity and digestive patterns above.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### mushroom

**Mushroom** — _PRODUCE_ (fungus).

**Benefits.** Mushrooms occupy their own kingdom and contribute things plants can't — meaningful protein per calorie, ergothioneine (a long-lived antioxidant concentrated in the fungal kingdom), beta-glucans (immune-modulating fibres), and vitamin D when exposed to UV light. Cohort data associate regular mushroom consumption with lower all-cause mortality in some longitudinal studies. Longo's Mediterranean tradition uses porcini, cremini, and field mushrooms in pasta, risotto, and braises; Blueprint uses cremini and shiitake as a meat-replacer for umami depth.

**How to consume.** Sauté in olive oil over high heat to drive off water and concentrate flavour — don't crowd the pan. Avoid prolonged boiling, which extracts the water-soluble compounds into liquid you may discard. Leave fresh mushrooms in sunlight for 15–30 minutes before cooking to lift the vitamin D yield. Dried porcini rehydrate into both a deep stock and a meaty texture.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine inside the eating window. Both reference patterns treat mushrooms as a near-weekly staple.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — mushrooms are a culinary food rather than an essential. Diets devoid of fungi miss the ergothioneine and beta-glucan contributions, which cohort data tie to inflammatory and immune markers over years.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake from cultivated mushrooms. Wild-foraging without expertise is the real risk vector here — misidentification can be lethal.

### fava-bean

**Fava bean (broad bean)** — _PRODUCE_ (fresh pulse).

**Benefits.** Fava is a true protein-bearing pulse, fresh in spring and dried year-round, with a strong fibre and folate profile, useful iron and magnesium, and a meaningful protein-per-calorie ratio. Longo's Sardinian and Calabrian traditions treat fresh fava as a spring staple — eaten raw with pecorino, or simmered into a purée (fave e cicoria). The bean's L-dopa content has been studied in Parkinson's research at therapeutic doses; at culinary intake the effect is negligible. Note: a small minority of people with G6PD deficiency must avoid fava entirely (favism) — a genetic condition worth being aware of in some Mediterranean populations.

**How to consume.** Fresh-podded fava can be eaten raw when young with pecorino and olive oil; older beans want their second skin slipped off after a brief blanch. Dried fava soaks overnight then simmers into purées and soups. Pair with bitter greens — fava and chicory is the Pugliese pattern that pulls fibre, protein, and bitter-leaf benefits into one dish.

**When to consume.** Fresh fava in spring, dried fava year-round, inside the eating window. A pulse-anchor meal at lunch fits Longo's pattern especially well.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own category — falls under the wider "diet chronically low in pulses" pattern, which cohort data on Mediterranean and Okinawan populations associate with worse longevity outcomes.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake outside the G6PD case. Heavy pulse intake with low hydration can produce GI discomfort patterns until the gut microbiome adapts.

### lamon-bean

**Lamon / borlotti bean** — _PRODUCE_ (fresh pulse).

**Benefits.** Lamon is the prized borlotti-style fresh bean from Belluno in the Veneto — a true protein-bearing pulse with the pink-and-white speckled pod that turns uniformly cream-coloured when cooked. Higher protein-per-calorie than green beans or snap peas by a wide margin, with the full pulse profile of fibre, folate, iron, magnesium, and resistant starch. Longo's Veneto tradition holds lamon in special regard — pasta e fagioli with lamon is the canonical use. The fresh form has a more delicate texture than the dried, with a shorter cooking time.

**How to consume.** Fresh-shelled lamon simmers in 30–45 minutes with a piece of onion, a celery stalk, and a bay leaf — no overnight soak needed. Dried lamon takes the standard pulse treatment: overnight soak, then a slow simmer with aromatics. Pair with pasta, with bitter greens, or alone with olive oil, garlic, and rosemary. The bean liquor (the cooking water) is itself a useful stock — don't drain and discard it.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window. A bowl of pasta e fagioli is a complete Longo-pattern meal anchor — pulse protein, slow carbohydrate, olive oil, herbs.

**Deficiency signs.** Same wider "diet chronically low in pulses" pattern — cohort data on Blue Zones populations tie low pulse intake to worse longevity markers.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Gas patterns ease as the gut microbiome adapts to regular pulse intake.

### green-bean

**Green bean** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Green beans are the immature whole pod of common-bean cultivars — eaten pod and all before the bean inside swells. Nutritionally they are mostly water and fibre with modest vitamin C, vitamin K, folate, and small carotenoids. They are *not* a protein-bearing pulse in the way fava or lamon are — the seed is too immature to carry meaningful protein. Useful as a vegetable, not as a protein source. Both reference patterns use them as a side or as a salad component.

**How to consume.** Blanch briefly in salted water then plunge in ice water to preserve colour and snap, or roast at high heat with olive oil until lightly charred. Avoid the long-boiled mushy treatment that destroys both texture and vitamin C. Pair with garlic, lemon, and almonds (the classic French side) or with tomato (Greek fasolakia).

**When to consume.** Late spring through summer, inside the eating window. A reliable side-vegetable that pairs with almost any main.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own category — falls under the wider "low vegetable volume" pattern.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### mung-bean-sprout

**Mung bean sprout** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Mung bean sprouts are the germinated form of dried mung beans — eaten as the white, crunchy bean sprout common in East Asian and Mediterranean salads. Sprouting raises vitamin C, folate, and some B-vitamins relative to the dried bean, and lowers anti-nutrient (phytate) content. Like green beans, sprouts are mostly water and fibre — *not* a protein-bearing pulse despite the bean parentage, because they are eaten at a stage when the bean's protein has been converted into the energy of germination. Useful for crunch and freshness, not for protein.

**How to consume.** Raw in salads, briefly stir-fried over high heat (longer cooking turns them limp), or wilted into noodle and rice bowls at the end of cooking. Rinse thoroughly — sprouts have a history of bacterial-contamination outbreaks, and rinsing or briefly cooking lowers the risk. Buy fresh and use within a couple of days; they don't keep.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window. A useful raw-vegetable crunch in salads year-round.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. The food-safety concern around raw sprouts is real for immunocompromised people — light cooking removes it.

### sugar-snap-pea

**Sugar snap pea** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Sugar snap peas are the whole edible pod of a sweet pea cultivar — eaten pod and all while the peas inside are still small and tender. Mostly water and fibre, with useful vitamin C, vitamin K, folate, and a touch of plant protein (more than green beans, less than fava). Like green beans, they sit on the vegetable side of the pulse / vegetable line — *not* a true protein-bearing pulse. Their sweetness comes from natural sugars that haven't yet converted to starch as the pea matures.

**How to consume.** Raw out of hand, briefly blanched and shocked for salads, or stir-fried for 60–90 seconds over high heat. Overcooking turns them dull and stringy. The fibrous string along the seam pulls off cleanly when you snap the stem end. Pair with sesame, ginger, lemon, or mint.

**When to consume.** Spring and early summer, inside the eating window. A reliable raw-snack vegetable that earns its place alongside cucumber and bell pepper in the afternoon habit.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### artichoke

**Artichoke** — _PRODUCE_.

**Benefits.** Artichoke is an edible thistle with a distinctive bitter-aromatic profile, and one of the best dietary sources of inulin (the prebiotic fibre also in chicories) and cynarin (a polyphenol studied for its bile-stimulating and liver-supporting properties). Folate, vitamin K, magnesium, and potassium are all meaningfully present. Longo's Roman tradition treats artichokes with reverence — carciofi alla romana (braised) and carciofi alla giudia (Roman-Jewish fried) are spring staples. Blueprint uses jarred artichoke hearts as a year-round pantry vegetable.

**How to consume.** Fresh young artichokes can be trimmed and braised whole in olive oil, garlic, mint, and white wine. Older or larger artichokes want their tough outer leaves stripped, the choke scooped out, and the heart used. Jarred or frozen artichoke hearts in olive oil are a reasonable shortcut for everyday cooking. Acidulate cut surfaces with lemon to stop browning. The bitter compounds prime digestion — eat early in a meal, not last.

**When to consume.** Spring (fresh) and year-round (preserved), inside the eating window. Works as antipasto, side, or pasta component.

**Deficiency signs.** Not its own category. Falls under the wider "diet chronically low in bitter and prebiotic-fibre foods" pattern, which cohort data tie to lower gut microbial diversity.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. The inulin can produce gas patterns in people not yet adapted to prebiotic-fibre intake.

### apple

**Apple** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Apples carry pectin (a soluble fibre that ferments to short-chain fatty acids and supports cholesterol metabolism), quercetin (a flavonoid concentrated in the skin), vitamin C, and small amounts of potassium. The skin holds most of the polyphenols and fibre — peeling discards the better half. Longo's tradition treats fresh fruit as the daily dessert, with apple as the year-round anchor; Blueprint includes apple in its daily fruit mix. Cohort data on apple intake tie regular consumption to lower cardiovascular and respiratory mortality markers.

**How to consume.** Whole, with skin on, as a snack or dessert. Pair with a fat / protein (nut butter, a handful of almonds, a slice of cheese) to flatten the glucose curve. Apple juice strips out the fibre and concentrates the sugar — it is not a substitute for the whole fruit. Baked apple with cinnamon is a clean dessert format.

**When to consume.** Daily inside the eating window. An apple in the afternoon is the canonical Mediterranean-pattern snack and fits Longo's 12h window comfortably.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in whole fresh fruit track in cohort data with lower fibre intake, lower polyphenol intake, and the cardiovascular markers tied to both.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Very heavy daily intake can contribute to GI gas patterns in people sensitive to fructose or sorbitol.

### pear

**Pear** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Pears are nutritionally close to apples — pectin, vitamin C, potassium, copper, and skin-concentrated polyphenols — with a slightly higher fibre-per-fruit count and a softer texture that some people tolerate more easily. The skin again holds most of the value. Longo's autumn and winter tradition pairs pears with walnut and aged cheese (the Tuscan "non far sapere al contadino quanto è buono il formaggio con le pere" saying captures the pattern). Blueprint includes pear in its rotational fruit mix.

**How to consume.** Whole, with skin on, ripe but firm. Pair with walnut, pecorino, or gorgonzola for the classic Mediterranean dessert format. Poached pear in red wine is a clean dessert that fits both reference patterns when sugar is kept minimal. Avoid pear juice and pear-nectar drinks for the same reason as apple juice.

**When to consume.** Autumn and winter (peak season) and year-round (storage varieties), inside the eating window. A reliable daily fruit alongside apple in the cooler months.

**Deficiency signs.** Same whole-fruit pattern as apple — diets chronically low in fresh fruit track with lower fibre and polyphenol intake in long observation.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. The sorbitol content is higher than in apple, which can drive GI patterns in sensitive people at very heavy intake.
