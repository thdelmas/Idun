### chestnut-dried

**Dried chestnut** — _LEGUMES_GRAINS_PASTA_.

**Benefits.** Chestnuts are the outlier of the nut family — low in fat, high in slow-burning starch, and the only common nut that behaves nutritionally more like a grain. Dried chestnuts concentrate that starch alongside potassium, magnesium, manganese, and a modest fibre payload. They are naturally gluten-free, which is why mountain cuisines from the Apennines to Corsica leaned on them for centuries as a staple flour and pot food. Longo's tradition reaches for chestnuts as a Mediterranean autumn carbohydrate; Blueprint doesn't feature them explicitly, but they fit the whole-food, low-glycemic-index pattern.

**How to consume.** Soak dried chestnuts overnight, then simmer 45–60 minutes until tender — they finish softer and sweeter than canned. Use them whole in stews and soups (the classic pairing is with cavolo nero, borlotti beans, and a parmesan rind), pureed into autumn vegetable soups, or ground into flour for flatbreads and pancakes. Their sweetness pairs well with bay leaf, rosemary, and a finishing drizzle of EVOO. Don't skip the soak; dried chestnuts cooked from hard are mealy.

**When to consume.** Seasonal — autumn and winter, when their warming starch fits the pattern. Inside Longo's 12h eating window like any other carbohydrate. A 50–80 g cooked portion sits comfortably alongside legumes and greens as a complete plate.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category on its own. Diets chronically low in whole-food complex carbohydrates — chestnuts, intact grains, tubers — often track with low energy on long days and the blood-sugar swings that come from leaning on refined starches instead.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Like any starchy food, very large portions can push past energy balance if they replace rather than complement vegetables and legumes.

### spelt

**Spelt** — _LEGUMES_GRAINS_PASTA_.

**Benefits.** Spelt is an ancient relative of modern wheat that kept a tougher hull and a less-bred genome through the centuries. Compared to common wheat it carries slightly more protein, more zinc and magnesium, and a fibre profile that tends to feel gentler on the gut — though it still contains gluten and is not safe for coeliacs. Whole-grain spelt (farro) is the form Longo's tradition treats as a Mediterranean longevity grain, eaten in soups, salads, and pilafs across central Italy. Blueprint uses whole grains in the same spirit but doesn't single spelt out.

**How to consume.** Cook whole spelt berries (farro) like rice — 25–35 minutes simmered in salted water until chewy but tender; pearled spelt is faster. Use cold in grain salads with chickpeas, parsley, lemon, and EVOO, or hot in the Tuscan **zuppa di farro** with borlotti beans and cavolo nero. Spelt flour bakes into denser, nuttier loaves than 00 wheat flour and works well at a 30–50% blend with wheat for sourdough.

**When to consume.** A regular grain rotation alongside brown rice, barley, and oats hits the whole-grain target both reference patterns push. Inside Longo's 12h eating window, paired with legumes and a vegetable.

**Deficiency signs.** No spelt-specific deficiency. Diets chronically low in whole, intact grains tend to track with lower fibre, lower magnesium, and the gut-microbiome thinning that comes with a refined-carb pattern.

**Excess signs.** Spelt contains gluten — people with coeliac disease or non-coeliac gluten sensitivity will react to it. Beyond that, no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### rice-brown

**Brown / semi-brown rice** — _LEGUMES_GRAINS_PASTA_.

**Benefits.** Brown rice keeps the bran and germ that white milling strips away, which is where most of the fibre, magnesium, manganese, and B-vitamin payload lives. The glycemic load is meaningfully lower than white rice for the same cooked weight, and the fibre slows the glucose curve enough that brown rice fits a longevity carbohydrate pattern where white rice doesn't quite. Both Blueprint and Longo treat whole grains as the default; semi-brown (partially milled) is a reasonable middle ground when full brown rice feels too dense.

**How to consume.** Rinse, then simmer 35–45 minutes in roughly 2:1 water to rice until tender — much longer than white rice, and worth the wait. A pinch of salt and a bay leaf in the water lifts the flavour. Pairs naturally with legumes (the rice-and-beans combination delivers a complete amino-acid profile), roasted vegetables, and a finishing splash of tamari or EVOO. Batch-cook and refrigerate; cooled and reheated rice carries more resistant starch, which feeds gut bacteria.

**When to consume.** A staple carbohydrate, eaten in the eating window. Roughly 60–80 g dry per portion is a comfortable plate size. Both reference patterns rotate it freely across the week.

**Deficiency signs.** No rice-specific deficiency. Diets chronically low in whole grains track with lower fibre intake and the slower gut transit that often comes with that pattern.

**Excess signs.** Brown rice — and rice broadly — accumulates arsenic from the soil more than most grains. The cohort signal is small at normal intakes; rinsing thoroughly and cooking in excess water (then draining) reduces it further. Rotating rice with spelt, barley, oats, and quinoa avoids leaning on any one grain.

### pasta-wheat

**Wheat pasta** — _LEGUMES_GRAINS_PASTA_.

**Benefits.** Pasta made from durum-wheat semolina has a denser protein matrix than soft-wheat noodles, which slows starch release and gives it a lower glycemic load than bread of equivalent weight — especially when cooked **al dente**. That structural quirk is part of why the Mediterranean pattern can lean on pasta several times a week without the metabolic cost a refined-starch diet would carry. Longo treats pasta as a central plate of the longevity diet; Blueprint doesn't feature pasta directly but tolerates it inside a whole-grain frame.

**How to consume.** Cook **al dente** — firm to the bite — in heavily salted water, then finish in the sauce rather than dressing it on the plate. Pair with legumes (pasta e ceci, pasta e fagioli), vegetables (pasta with broccoli, with cavolo nero, with tomato and basil), and EVOO; the longevity move is to treat pasta as the carbohydrate scaffold for a bean-and-vegetable plate, not as a standalone dish. Whole-wheat pasta swaps in some bran and germ at the cost of texture; it's a reasonable rotation.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window. Longo's tradition puts pasta at lunch more often than dinner — daytime carbohydrate sits more comfortably with circadian metabolism. A 70–80 g dry portion is the Mediterranean default.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Diets that strip carbohydrates entirely often track with low energy and disrupted sleep over the long term.

**Excess signs.** Pasta carries gluten and is a refined-ish starch relative to whole-grain berries. Very large portions, eaten daily without legumes or vegetables, push the same refined-carb pattern any other white flour would.

### flour-wheat

**Wheat flour (00 / soft / whole)** — _FLOURS_BREAD_.

**Benefits.** Wheat flour is a spectrum, not a single ingredient. **Whole-wheat flour** keeps the bran (fibre, B vitamins, minerals) and the germ (vitamin E, healthy fats), which is where the nutritional payload lives. **00 / soft / refined flour** strips both, leaving mostly starch and gluten — fluffier crumb, longer shelf life, far less fibre and micronutrient density. Both reference patterns favour the whole form; Longo's tradition tolerates refined flour in pasta and pizza dough where structure demands it, and Blueprint sticks to whole grains.

**How to consume.** For everyday baking — flatbreads, pancakes, rustic loaves — use whole-wheat or at least a 50/50 blend with white. Reserve 00 for dishes where its low protein and fine grind genuinely matter (fresh egg pasta, Neapolitan pizza). Whole-wheat flour goes rancid faster because of the germ's fats; store it in the fridge or freezer if you bake slowly. Hydrate whole-wheat doughs a touch more than white; the bran drinks water.

**When to consume.** A daily-baking staple in the eating window. The refinement level matters more than the timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Diets built almost entirely on refined wheat — white bread, white pasta, pastries — track with lower fibre intake, lower B-vitamin and mineral density, and the flatter satiety curve that often goes with refined-carb-heavy patterns.

**Excess signs.** Contains gluten — not safe for coeliacs and irritating for some with sensitivity. A diet dominated by refined wheat at the expense of vegetables, legumes, and whole grains is the recognisable Western pattern that the longevity literature pushes back against.

### flour-corn

**Corn / maize flour** — _FLOURS_BREAD_.

**Benefits.** Corn flour — the finely-ground whole kernel, including the bran and germ — is naturally gluten-free and carries a modest fibre payload alongside carotenoids (the yellow pigment), magnesium, and B vitamins. Stone-ground or **nixtamalised** corn flour (treated with alkaline lime, the masa harina tradition) liberates niacin that's otherwise bound and inaccessible, which is why corn-eating cultures historically nixtamalised. Neither Blueprint nor Longo singles corn out, but polenta sits inside Longo's northern-Italian tradition as a whole-grain rotation.

**How to consume.** Coarser grinds make polenta (slow-cooked, 30–45 minutes, stirred); finer grinds make flatbreads, cornbread, and tortillas. Pair with beans and vegetables to round out the amino-acid profile — corn-and-beans is one of the world's most-tested complementary protein pairings. A drizzle of EVOO and a shaving of aged parmesan finishes a polenta plate without crossing into heavy dairy. Choose stone-ground whole corn flour over **degerminated** versions; the germ holds the fat-soluble vitamins.

**When to consume.** A regular grain rotation, inside the eating window. Polenta works particularly well as a daytime carbohydrate base under stewed vegetables or legumes.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets historically over-reliant on **un-nixtamalised** corn (with little else) showed the classic niacin-deficiency pattern (pellagra); this is essentially absent in modern mixed diets. No corn-specific deficiency at culinary intake today.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Highly processed corn derivatives (high-fructose corn syrup, refined corn starch in ultra-processed foods) are a different conversation from whole corn flour.

### corn-starch

**Corn starch** — _FLOURS_BREAD_.

**Benefits.** Corn starch is the pure starch fraction of the corn kernel — bran and germ removed — used as a thickener for sauces, custards, and gluten-free baking. Nutritionally it carries almost nothing beyond energy: no meaningful fibre, no vitamins, no minerals. Its value is functional, not nutritional. Neither Blueprint nor Longo treats it as a featured ingredient; it appears in trace amounts in a handful of Longo's dessert and sauce recipes as a thickener.

**How to consume.** Slurry it in cold water before adding to hot liquid — added dry, it clumps. A teaspoon is usually enough to thicken a sauce for four; more turns it gluey. Corn starch loses thickening power when boiled too long or stirred too aggressively, so add at the end and ease off the heat. In gluten-free baking it lightens crumb when blended with rice flour and a binder.

**When to consume.** No timing concern — it's a culinary trace ingredient, not a food in its own right.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. As with any refined starch, it shouldn't displace whole-food carbohydrates in the everyday diet, but at the teaspoons-per-week level it's used as a thickener, the question doesn't really arise.

### semolina

**Durum-wheat semolina** — _FLOURS_BREAD_.

**Benefits.** Semolina is the coarsely-ground endosperm of **durum wheat**, the hard wheat that gives dried pasta its bite. Its higher protein content (mostly gluten) and denser starch matrix are exactly what slow the glycemic release in al-dente pasta. Compared to soft-wheat 00 flour, semolina carries slightly more protein and a touch more carotenoid (the yellow tint), though the bran and germ are still removed in standard semolina; **whole-grain durum** flour keeps them. Longo's tradition treats semolina pasta as a central longevity carbohydrate.

**How to consume.** The two classic uses are pasta-making (semolina dough holds shape under extrusion better than soft-wheat dough) and as a coating or thickener — dusting a pizza peel, thickening a North-African couscous, or finishing a gnocchi alla romana. Coarser semolina (**semola rimacinata** is the finer twice-milled grind) takes more water to hydrate than soft flour; let the dough rest 30 minutes before working it.

**When to consume.** In the eating window, as the carbohydrate base of a meal. Longo's pattern leans on it at lunch more than dinner.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Diets chronically low in any whole grain — semolina or otherwise — track with the lower-fibre pattern noted above.

**Excess signs.** Contains gluten. Beyond that, no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### bread

**Bread / breadcrumbs** — _FLOURS_BREAD_.

**Benefits.** Bread is the everyday vehicle for grain in both reference patterns, and the quality varies more than almost any other staple. **Whole-grain sourdough** retains bran, germ, and the fermentation-derived organic acids that lower the glycemic response and improve mineral bioavailability — the loaf the longevity literature actually points to. **White industrial sliced bread** is on the other end: refined flour, fast yeast, soft crumb, sharp glucose spike. Longo's tradition treats real bread (sourdough, whole-grain) as a daily food; Blueprint mostly skips bread, preferring intact whole grains.

**How to consume.** Choose long-fermented whole-grain or rye loaves where possible; a 24–48h sourdough rise meaningfully changes how the loaf behaves metabolically. Pair with EVOO and tomato, with hummus, with a bean soup — bread as the scaffold for a vegetable-and-legume plate rather than the meal itself. Stale bread becomes breadcrumbs (a Mediterranean thrift staple), thickener for ribollita, or the basis for pappa al pomodoro.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window. Daytime carbohydrate sits more comfortably with circadian metabolism than late-evening bread.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Diets that strip carbohydrates entirely often run into energy and sleep issues over the long term.

**Excess signs.** A diet heavy on white industrial bread is the recognisable refined-carb pattern: blood-sugar swings, lower satiety, lower fibre. Real fermented bread doesn't carry the same signature at culinary intake.

### taralli

**Taralli (Pugliese cracker)** — _FLOURS_BREAD_.

**Benefits.** Taralli are small ring-shaped crackers from Puglia made with flour, olive oil, white wine, and salt — sometimes with fennel seed or black pepper. They're a refined-flour snack, but the EVOO content and the boil-then-bake method give them a sturdier, less-processed character than industrial crackers. They appear in Longo's Mediterranean tradition as the everyday savoury snack alongside olives and a glass of wine.

**How to consume.** Eat them as they come — with olives, with a tomato-and-EVOO salad, alongside a vegetable antipasto. They keep for weeks in an airtight tin. The fennel-seed version pairs particularly well with raw vegetables and hummus.

**When to consume.** Snack scale, in the eating window. A small handful sits comfortably as an aperitivo accompaniment; they're not a meal in their own right.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Salt-forward and refined-flour-based — a daily large portion contributes to the salt and refined-carb load. Treat them as a snack rather than a staple.

### milk-coconut

**Coconut milk** — _MILKS_BROTHS_.

**Benefits.** Coconut milk is the pressed liquid of mature coconut flesh, rich in saturated fat — primarily medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs) like lauric acid — and the small payload of manganese and copper the flesh carries. The MCT profile metabolises faster than long-chain saturated fats, which is part of why coconut keeps showing up in keto and metabolic-flexibility contexts. Neither Blueprint nor Longo treats coconut milk as a featured longevity fat — both lean on EVOO instead — but it appears across both corpuses as a culinary fat for curries, dals, and creamy soups.

**How to consume.** **Full-fat canned coconut milk** is the cooking form; the watery cartoned drink labelled "coconut milk" is a different product (mostly water, see plant-based milk). Stir well before opening — the cream separates and floats. Use it to enrich soups (squash, lentil dal), curries, and rice dishes; the fat carries the spice. A small can usually feeds four; you don't need much for the texture.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window like any cooking fat. There's no fasting-window concern with fats.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Energy-dense and saturated-fat-heavy. Daily large portions push saturated-fat intake higher than either reference pattern targets — the MCT framing softens but doesn't erase that. Treat it as a flavour and texture tool, not a default cooking fat.

### milk-plant

**Plant-based milk (generic)** — _MILKS_BROTHS_.

**Benefits.** "Plant milk" covers a wide range: soy, almond, oat, hemp, pea, coconut-drink. Soy milk is the closest match to cow's milk on protein (~7–8 g per cup) and carries the isoflavones of the soybean; oat milk is starchier and carbohydrate-dominant; almond and coconut drinks are mostly water with a trace of the nut. The category is a useful dairy substitute in coffee, oatmeal, and baking — and Blueprint uses unsweetened plant milks routinely, since Blueprint omits dairy. Longo's tradition allows some dairy but tolerates plant milks freely.

**How to consume.** Choose **unsweetened** versions — the default supermarket plant milk is often sweetened with cane sugar or rice syrup. Read the label for added oils and emulsifiers; the cleaner products list the plant, water, and a pinch of salt. Soy and oat foam well for coffee; almond and rice tend to stay flat. Many plant milks are fortified with calcium, vitamin B12, and vitamin D — relevant for anyone on a plant-forward pattern.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window. No specific timing concern.

**Deficiency signs.** Plant milks themselves aren't a deficiency category. A plant-forward diet that swaps in unfortified almond or rice milk for dairy without compensating elsewhere can leave the B12 and calcium picture thin — fortified versions or other sources of those nutrients (nutritional yeast, leafy greens) cover the gap.

**Excess signs.** Sweetened plant milks contribute meaningful added sugar at daily volumes. Beyond that, no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake of the unsweetened forms.

### broth-vegetable

**Vegetable broth / stock** — _MILKS_BROTHS_.

**Benefits.** A simmered extract of onion, carrot, celery, herbs, and whatever vegetable trim the kitchen has on hand. Nutritionally light — most of the vitamin payload of the vegetables leaches into the water along with the flavour, but the calorie and macro density stays low. The real value is functional: a flavour base that lets you cook grains, legumes, and soups without leaning on salt or fat to carry taste. Longo's recipes use vegetable broth liberally; Blueprint uses it where a savoury liquid is needed.

**How to consume.** Homemade is best — simmer (don't boil) trim and aromatics for 45–90 minutes, strain, and refrigerate or freeze. A parmesan rind, a bay leaf, and a clove of garlic round it out. Store-bought is fine; check the salt level (the low-sodium versions give you control over the seasoning of whatever you're cooking). Use as the cooking liquid for rice, farro, lentils, and risotto; deglaze pans with it; build soups on top.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window. A warm cup of broth can also sit comfortably during the fasting window for some interpretations — it's nearly calorie-free — but a strict 12h water-only fast would exclude it.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category.

**Excess signs.** Salt level is the only practical concern; commercial broths range from gentle to very salty. Beyond that, no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### egg

**Egg** — _DAIRY_EGGS_.

**Benefits.** Eggs are one of the most complete single-food protein sources — all essential amino acids, plus choline (concentrated in the yolk and relevant for brain and liver metabolism), lutein and zeaxanthin (eye carotenoids), vitamin B12, vitamin D, and selenium. The yolk carries the bulk of the micronutrients and the cholesterol; the white is almost pure protein. **Blueprint explicitly excludes eggs** — Bryan Johnson's protocol skips them on a methionine-restriction and cholesterol argument. **Longo's Mediterranean variant allows a few eggs a week**, treating them as a moderate-frequency protein source. Both lenses are defensible; the choice is yours.

**How to consume.** Soft-boiled, poached, or gently scrambled preserves the most heat-sensitive nutrients in the yolk; hard-boiled is fine but oxidises some yolk fats. Pair with vegetables, whole grains, and a fat (olive oil) rather than with bacon and white toast — the company eggs keep matters as much as the egg itself. Choose pasture-raised or omega-3-enriched eggs where available; the fatty-acid and carotenoid profile is meaningfully different.

**When to consume.** If you follow Longo's allowance, 2–4 a week is the tradition's rough range — typically at breakfast or lunch inside the eating window. If you follow Blueprint, none.

**Deficiency signs.** Eggs aren't an essential food, so "egg deficiency" doesn't exist as such. Diets chronically low in choline-rich foods (eggs, soy, certain organ meats) can show the choline gap in cohort data; plant-forward eaters typically cover it through soy and cruciferous vegetables.

**Excess signs.** Cohort data on eggs and cardiovascular outcomes is mixed and contested — the older "eggs spike cholesterol" framing has softened, but the signal isn't zero either. Daily 3–4 eggs over years sits outside both reference patterns; a few a week is where the Mediterranean tradition lands.

### cheese-parmesan

**Aged parmesan (Grana Padano / Parmigiano)** — _DAIRY_EGGS_.

**Benefits.** Aged hard cheeses like Parmigiano-Reggiano and Grana Padano are nutrient-dense in protein and calcium per gram, fermented long enough (12–36 months) that the lactose is essentially gone, and carry the umami-glutamate profile that makes them a finishing flavour rather than a bulk ingredient. **Longo's tradition treats aged cheese as a small finishing condiment** — a tablespoon shaved over pasta or soup — not as a protein source. **Blueprint omits dairy entirely**, including parmesan. Both lenses sit on the same fact: parmesan delivers flavour density that lets you use very little.

**How to consume.** Grate or shave fresh from a wedge; pre-grated bagged parmesan is usually a different product (often blended with cellulose). A teaspoon to a tablespoon finishes a bowl of pasta, soup, or risotto. The rind goes into bean soups and broths to add savoury depth — a Mediterranean thrift move worth keeping. Don't cook parmesan into a sauce over high heat; it clumps and turns grainy.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window, as a finishing flavour rather than a daily-volume food. If you follow Longo, small daily amounts fit; if you follow Blueprint, skip.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Diets that omit dairy entirely (Blueprint's lens) need to source calcium and B12 elsewhere — fortified plant milks, leafy greens, sardines, nutritional yeast all cover the gap.

**Excess signs.** Salt-forward and saturated-fat-dense. Daily large portions (50 g+) push both the salt and the saturated-fat load; the Mediterranean tradition uses it by the teaspoon for a reason.

### cheese-ricotta-salata

**Ricotta salata** — _DAIRY_EGGS_.

**Benefits.** Ricotta salata is ricotta that's been salted, pressed, and aged — a firm, mild, sliceable cheese from southern Italy, more delicate than feta and less assertive than aged pecorino. The aging concentrates protein and salt; calcium follows. Like all aged cheese in **Longo's tradition, it's a flavour finish, not a protein source** — shaved over pasta with eggplant (pasta alla Norma), crumbled onto salads, layered into baked vegetable dishes. **Blueprint omits dairy entirely.**

**How to consume.** Slice or crumble directly onto the finished dish; cooking it dulls the texture that makes it useful. Pairs particularly well with tomato, eggplant, and basil — the southern-Italian flavour triangle. A tablespoon or two finishes a plate; more turns the dish into a cheese plate. Store wrapped in parchment in the fridge.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window. A small Mediterranean amount across the week, if dairy is in your pattern.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Same calcium / B12 caveat as parmesan for dairy-free patterns.

**Excess signs.** Salt-forward — the **salata** is literally "salted". Daily large portions contribute to the salt load. Beyond that, no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### almond

**Almond** — _NUTS_SEEDS_.

**Benefits.** Almonds carry monounsaturated fat (similar profile to olive oil), the highest vitamin E density of any common nut, magnesium, and a fibre payload that keeps the glycemic response gentle. Cohort data tie regular almond intake to favourable lipid markers and steadier post-meal glucose. **Blueprint** features almonds and almond flour across the daily protocol; **Longo's tradition** treats them as one of the Mediterranean default snacks. The skin holds most of the polyphenols, so leave it on when you can.

**How to consume.** A small handful (~25–30 g, roughly 20–25 nuts) is the everyday portion. Raw or dry-roasted; skip the heavily salted and oil-roasted versions where the oil is usually a refined seed oil. Soaking overnight softens the texture and is the traditional move in Mediterranean and Ayurvedic kitchens; it doesn't meaningfully change the nutritional profile but makes them easier on the gut for some people. Almond butter and almond flour extend the same payload to baking and spreads.

**When to consume.** Daily, inside the eating window. Either as a snack or worked into meals (over salads, into oatmeal, in baked goods).

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Diets chronically low in nuts broadly track in cohort data with worse cardiovascular markers — a low-nut pattern is one of the more reliable signals in the longevity literature.

**Excess signs.** Energy-dense — a large daily handful (>60–80 g) adds up. Almonds also carry oxalates; people prone to calcium-oxalate kidney stones may want to keep the daily portion moderate.

### hazelnut

**Hazelnut** — _NUTS_SEEDS_.

**Benefits.** Hazelnuts sit in the same Mediterranean-nut family as almonds — high in monounsaturated fat, generous in vitamin E and manganese, with a polyphenol profile concentrated in the skin. They're a defining ingredient of northern-Italian cuisine (Piedmont in particular) and appear across Longo's tradition in pestos, sauces, and desserts. Blueprint doesn't single hazelnuts out but treats them inside the daily nut rotation.

**How to consume.** Toast briefly (8–10 minutes at 160 °C) to develop the flavour, then rub off the papery skins in a clean tea towel if a dish calls for it — though the skin holds most of the polyphenols, so leave it on for everyday eating. Use whole in salads and baked goods, ground into nut butters, or worked into a pesto with parsley and EVOO. Pair with dark chocolate (the Piedmontese **gianduja** tradition) for a longevity-friendly dessert.

**When to consume.** Daily, inside the eating window, as part of a small mixed-nut handful. There's no specific best time of day.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Same low-nut-diet pattern noted for almonds applies here.

**Excess signs.** Energy-dense like all nuts. No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### walnut

**Walnut** — _NUTS_SEEDS_.

**Benefits.** Walnuts are the standout plant-based source of **alpha-linolenic acid (ALA)**, the short-chain omega-3 fatty acid — alongside flax and chia, they're the trio the longevity literature points to for plant omega-3. They also carry the highest polyphenol load of any common nut and a mineral profile (magnesium, copper, manganese) that mirrors the broader nut family. **Blueprint** features walnuts in the daily nut mix; **Longo's tradition** uses them in pestos, sauces, and as a snack across Mediterranean tables.

**How to consume.** Eat raw or lightly toasted; high-heat roasting damages the delicate ALA in the oil. A small daily handful (~25–30 g, 7–10 halves) is the cohort-study dose that keeps showing up in favourable cardiovascular signals. Pair with apple and a shaving of aged cheese for a classic plate, fold into oatmeal, or grind into a walnut-and-parsley pesto. Store in the fridge or freezer — the ALA fats go rancid faster than monounsaturated nut oils.

**When to consume.** Daily, inside the eating window.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in ALA omega-3s — walnut, flax, chia, hemp — track with the broader low-omega-3 pattern (drier skin, the inflammation-marker signal in cohort data). Walnuts, flax, and chia together are the practical plant route to closing that gap.

**Excess signs.** Energy-dense. No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### pine-nut

**Pine nut** — _NUTS_SEEDS_.

**Benefits.** Pine nuts are the seed of stone-pine cones, harvested by hand from Mediterranean and Asian species — which is most of why they're expensive. Nutritionally they sit in the monounsaturated-fat band of the nut family, with a notable magnesium and manganese payload and the pinolenic-acid signature (a fatty acid specific to pine seeds, associated in small studies with satiety signalling). **Longo's tradition** uses them in pesto, in stuffings, and across Italian sauces; Blueprint doesn't feature them specifically.

**How to consume.** Toast briefly in a dry pan over low heat — they burn fast — until just golden. Use in pesto (the classic basil-pine-nut-parmesan-garlic-EVOO build), scattered over salads, in cookies, or with raisins in Sicilian sweet-and-sour vegetable dishes. Store in the fridge or freezer; the oils go rancid quickly. A small handful is plenty.

**When to consume.** Inside the eating window, as part of meals more often than as a standalone snack — they're priced more like a flavour ingredient than a daily nut.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category on their own.

**Excess signs.** **Pine-mouth** — a temporary metallic bitter aftertaste that can last days — is a known reaction to certain pine-nut species, particularly some Chinese imports. It's harmless and resolves on its own. Beyond that, no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### pistachio

**Pistachio** — _NUTS_SEEDS_.

**Benefits.** Pistachios carry the same monounsaturated-fat backbone as the rest of the Mediterranean-nut family, with one of the higher protein densities among nuts and a meaningful payload of lutein and zeaxanthin (the same eye-carotenoids found in egg yolks). They also bring B6, copper, and a generous fibre fraction. **Blueprint** uses pistachios in the daily nut rotation; **Longo's tradition** treats them as a Sicilian and Mediterranean staple, in pestos, sauces, and desserts.

**How to consume.** Buy them in the shell when you can — shelling slows the eating pace and tends to land you on the right portion (a small handful, ~25–30 g of shelled weight). Choose unsalted or lightly salted; the heavily salted bar-snack versions push the daily salt load fast. Use ground in Sicilian pesto with EVOO and parmesan, scattered over salads and yoghurt, or in baked goods.

**When to consume.** Daily, inside the eating window. A handful as a mid-afternoon snack fits the Mediterranean rhythm.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. The broader low-nut-diet pattern noted under almonds applies here.

**Excess signs.** Energy-dense. Heavily salted versions contribute meaningfully to the salt load at daily large portions. No other commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### chia-seed

**Chia seed** — _NUTS_SEEDS_.

**Benefits.** Chia seeds are one of the densest plant sources of **alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) omega-3**, alongside flax and walnut. They carry a remarkable soluble-fibre payload — the mucilage that swells in water and forms the chia-pudding gel — along with calcium, magnesium, and complete plant protein. The soluble fibre slows gastric emptying and the glucose curve of the meal it's eaten with. **Blueprint** features chia in the daily protocol; **Longo's tradition** doesn't single chia out (it's a New-World food, outside the Mediterranean canon), but it fits the longevity pattern cleanly.

**How to consume.** Whole chia is fine — unlike flax, the seed coat is thin enough that you absorb most of the payload without grinding. Soak 1–2 tablespoons in liquid (water, plant milk, yoghurt) for 10–15 minutes until it gels; eat as a pudding, stir into oatmeal, or use as an egg substitute in baking (1 tbsp chia + 3 tbsp water = 1 egg). Drink water with chia — the soluble fibre needs hydration to do its work.

**When to consume.** Daily, inside the eating window. Many people eat it at breakfast for the slow-release glucose curve.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in plant ALA omega-3s — chia, flax, walnut, hemp — track with the broader low-omega-3 pattern noted under walnut.

**Excess signs.** Large dry portions without enough water can swell in the throat or gut and cause discomfort — always soak or eat with plenty of liquid. Beyond that, no commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### flax-seed

**Flax seed** — _NUTS_SEEDS_.

**Benefits.** Flax is the third member of the plant ALA omega-3 trio (with walnut and chia), and the most concentrated of them by weight. It also carries **lignans** — phytoestrogens with their own cohort-data signal on hormonal cancers — and a heavy soluble-and-insoluble fibre mix that supports gut transit and steadier post-meal glucose. **Blueprint** features ground flax daily; **Longo's tradition** uses it less prominently but it fits the longevity pattern.

**How to consume.** **Grind it.** Whole flax seeds pass through the gut intact and you absorb very little of the omega-3 or lignan payload. Buy whole, grind a week's worth at a time, and store the meal in an airtight container in the fridge or freezer — ground flax oxidises fast. A tablespoon a day is the cohort-study dose. Stir into oatmeal, yoghurt, smoothies, or baking; the gel it forms in water also works as an egg substitute (1 tbsp ground flax + 3 tbsp water = 1 egg).

**When to consume.** Daily, inside the eating window. No specific best time of day.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in plant ALA omega-3s show the broader pattern noted under walnut and chia.

**Excess signs.** Very large daily portions (>3–4 tbsp) can push the fibre load high enough to cause gut discomfort; introduce gradually if you're starting from a low-fibre baseline. The lignan dose at culinary intake is small and not a concern, though anyone on hormone-sensitive medication may want to flag it to a clinician.

### hemp-seed

**Hemp seed** — _NUTS_SEEDS_.

**Benefits.** Hulled hemp hearts are one of the few plant foods that deliver complete protein (all essential amino acids) at a meaningful density — roughly 10 g of protein in 3 tablespoons — alongside a balanced omega-3 / omega-6 ratio (more omega-6 than walnut or flax, but in a ratio cohort data treats as favourable). They also bring magnesium, iron, and zinc. **Blueprint** uses hemp hearts in the daily protocol; **Longo's tradition** doesn't feature them historically, but they fit the longevity pattern.

**How to consume.** Hulled hearts are ready to eat raw — no soaking, grinding, or toasting needed. A 2–3 tablespoon daily portion sprinkled over salads, oatmeal, yoghurt, or blended into smoothies covers the typical use. The flavour is mild and grassy, which is why they blend into other foods easily rather than standing alone. Store in the fridge; the fats oxidise faster than most seeds.

**When to consume.** Daily, inside the eating window.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category on their own. Plant-forward diets that don't cover plant protein from beans, lentils, soy, and seeds together can show a thin protein picture; hemp helps close that gap.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Hemp hearts contain only trace cannabinoids — well below any psychoactive threshold — and don't show up in routine drug screening at normal food doses.
