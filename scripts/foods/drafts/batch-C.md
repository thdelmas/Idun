### lemon

**Lemon** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Lemons are a vitamin-C anchor and a polyphenol carrier — the zest and pith hold most of the flavonoids (eriocitrin, hesperidin) and the limonene that gives the rind its bright nose. Longo uses lemon as a finishing acid across vegetable and seafood dishes; the citric acid also helps non-heme iron absorption when squeezed onto leafy greens or legumes. The juice alone delivers vitamin C, but the rind is where the more interesting bioactive payload sits.

**How to consume.** Zest before juicing — the outermost yellow layer is the polyphenol-dense part, and discarding it is the most common kitchen waste in the lemon. Squeeze raw onto cooked greens, beans, fish, or soup at the table rather than during cooking; vitamin C degrades with prolonged heat. A wedge in water, lemon dressings, preserved-lemon pastes, and gremolata (zest + parsley + garlic) all keep the rind in play. Buy unwaxed / organic when you plan to use the zest.

**When to consume.** Daily is reasonable. Both reference patterns lean on citrus across the eating window; there's no specific timing constraint. Some people find very acidic citrus harsh on an empty stomach — pair with food if so.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in vitamin-C-bearing produce track in cohort data with slower wound healing, bleeding gums, and persistent low energy. The "scurvy spectrum" is rare in modern diets but the sub-clinical end of it shows up where fresh fruit is absent for long stretches.

**Excess signs.** Heavy daily raw-juice intake can erode tooth enamel over time and aggravate reflux. No commonly-reported systemic excess pattern at culinary intake.

### lime

**Lime** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Lime sits next to lemon nutritionally — vitamin C, citric acid, and a flavonoid/limonene profile concentrated in the zest. It carries slightly different aromatics (more terpene-forward), which is why it dominates the herb-and-chili kitchens of Latin America and Southeast Asia where lemon would feel out of place. Neither Blueprint nor Longo treats lime as a daily staple, but it's a useful rotation partner for lemon and a gentle way to brighten dishes without salt.

**How to consume.** Zest first, juice second — the green outer skin holds the limonene and the flavonoids. Finish raw onto avocado, beans, fish tacos, or chopped herbs; the juice goes in last so the vitamin C survives. Lime pairs naturally with cilantro and chili (a near-universal triplet across cuisines). Buy unwaxed when using the zest.

**When to consume.** Whenever it fits the dish — no timing constraint. A daily squeeze of lime or lemon onto a meal is a low-cost way to keep vitamin C and citrus flavonoids in the diet.

**Deficiency signs.** Same pattern as lemon — diets chronically low in fresh citrus and other vitamin-C-bearing produce track with slower healing, dull skin, and bleeding gums at the sub-clinical end.

**Excess signs.** Raw juice in volume can erode tooth enamel and aggravate reflux. No commonly-reported systemic excess pattern at culinary intake.

### orange

**Orange** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Oranges deliver vitamin C, folate, potassium, and a substantial dose of hesperidin — a flavonoid concentrated in the white pith and the zest that's associated with vascular and blood-pressure markers in cohort studies. The whole fruit also brings fibre, which slows the sugar release and is the main argument for eating an orange rather than drinking orange juice. Longo's pattern treats whole citrus as a daily fruit option; Blueprint uses oranges less, leaning more on berries.

**How to consume.** Eat the segments with as much of the white pith as you can stand — that's where hesperidin sits, alongside the soluble fibre. Zest goes into dressings, marinades, and baked dishes. Orange juice, even fresh-squeezed, strips out the fibre and concentrates the sugar — fine as an occasional drink, not as a daily hydration habit.

**When to consume.** Daily fruit slot, inside the eating window. Some people find citrus on an empty stomach harsh — pair with breakfast or with nuts if so.

**Deficiency signs.** Diets chronically low in vitamin-C and folate-bearing fruits track in cohort data with persistent fatigue, slower healing, and dull skin. The flavonoid gap is harder to see at the individual level but shows up in cardiovascular markers at the population level.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern when eaten as whole fruit at culinary intake. Habitual large-volume juice intake is a different question — it can push sugar load and dental wear.

### blueberry

**Blueberry** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Blueberries are the anthocyanin-anchor of the Blueprint protocol — Bryan Johnson eats them daily, and the recipe corpus reflects that. The deep blue skin pigment is where most of the polyphenols sit, and these compounds are repeatedly associated with cognitive and vascular markers in cohort studies. Blueberries are also low on the glycemic load relative to most fruits, which is why Longo and Blueprint both accept them generously inside their patterns.

**How to consume.** Raw, frozen, or gently warmed — high heat (baking at 200°C for an hour) does degrade some of the anthocyanins, but the loss is modest. Frozen wild blueberries are often more pigment-dense than fresh cultivated ones, and they're cheaper year-round. Pair with nuts, oats, or yoghurt; the fat and protein blunt the sugar curve.

**When to consume.** Daily. A cup a day is the Blueprint dose; Longo's pattern is less specific but also accepts daily berries. There's no fasting-window concern — they sit inside the eating window with the meal they accompany.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category in the clinical sense. Diets chronically devoid of dark berries and other deeply-coloured fruit miss out on the anthocyanin/polyphenol family broadly, which tracks in cohort data with worse cognitive-aging markers.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Eaten by the kilo daily they're still a sugar load, but the typical bowl-a-day dose sits well inside normal patterns.

### strawberry

**Strawberry** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Strawberries combine vitamin C, manganese, and a distinct polyphenol set led by **ellagic acid** and **pelargonidin** — the red pigment carries most of the antioxidant payload. Among common berries they're particularly vitamin-C dense. Both Blueprint and Longo accept strawberries within the daily fruit slot; cohort data link regular berry intake to slower cognitive-aging markers.

**How to consume.** Raw is best — vitamin C and many of the polyphenols degrade with sustained heat. Eat with the green tops trimmed but not hollowed out; the area just under the cap is the most flavour-dense. Pair with nuts, yoghurt, or dark chocolate. Frozen strawberries retain most of their nutritional value and are useful out of season.

**When to consume.** Daily fruit slot, inside the eating window. No specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Diets chronically low in vitamin-C-bearing fruit track with the same patterns noted for citrus — slower healing, dull skin, persistent low energy.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. People with oral-allergy syndrome to birch pollen sometimes react to strawberries — a pattern worth noting but not a population-level concern.

### blackcurrant

**Blackcurrant** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Blackcurrants are among the most anthocyanin-dense berries in common cultivation — the black skin carries roughly four times the polyphenol load of blueberries by weight. They also deliver an unusually high vitamin C dose for a berry. Neither Blueprint nor Longo names them explicitly, but they sit comfortably inside both patterns as a polyphenol-dense rotation berry, particularly in European kitchens where they're more available.

**How to consume.** Raw if you can find them fresh; otherwise frozen, in jams with minimal added sugar, or as the unsweetened juice / cordial concentrate that's traditional in the UK and Scandinavia. The skin and seeds are where the value sits — don't strain. Pair with oats, yoghurt, or game meats.

**When to consume.** Daily fruit slot when in season; otherwise as a frozen rotation alongside blueberries and strawberries.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. Same broader pattern as the other dark berries — diets chronically devoid of anthocyanin-bearing fruit miss out on the polyphenol family.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### cherry-fresh

**Cherry** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Cherries carry anthocyanins (the red/black pigments), vitamin C, and — distinctively — measurable melatonin, particularly in tart varieties like Montmorency. Cohort and small-trial data associate tart-cherry intake with sleep quality and post-exercise recovery markers. Sweet cherries (Bing, Rainier) carry the anthocyanin payload but much less melatonin. Both Blueprint and Longo accept cherries within the daily fruit slot when in season.

**How to consume.** Raw, pitted, eaten by the handful. Tart cherries are usually consumed as unsweetened juice or dried; sweet cherries are a fresh-eating fruit. Frozen cherries are fine for smoothies and baking. Pair with nuts or dark chocolate.

**When to consume.** Daily fruit slot in season. Some people specifically take tart-cherry juice in the evening for the melatonin signal — a reasonable cue, though the effect is gentle.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. The broader anthocyanin/polyphenol gap noted for other dark berries applies.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Large volumes of cherry juice can act as a mild laxative due to the sorbitol content.

### fig-dried

**Fig (dried)** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Dried figs concentrate the calcium, potassium, magnesium, and fibre of the fresh fruit roughly five-fold by weight as water leaves. They're a useful plant-calcium source and a traditional Mediterranean winter sweet. Longo's pattern includes dried figs sparingly — as a dessert or a paired sweet with nuts, not as a daily handful — because the sugar concentration is high.

**How to consume.** Eat in small portions (2–3 figs), ideally paired with nuts (walnuts, almonds) or cheese to blunt the sugar curve and add fat and protein. The classic Mediterranean pairing is fig + walnut + a sliver of aged cheese. Chop into oats, yoghurt, or grain salads. Soak briefly in warm water if they've dried out hard.

**When to consume.** Sparingly. Once or twice a week in Longo's pattern, inside the eating window. The fresh fruit is a different proposition — eat that more freely when in season.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category specific to figs. Diets chronically low in plant-calcium sources (figs, sesame, leafy greens, almonds) track in cohort data with worse bone-density markers, particularly in people who don't consume dairy.

**Excess signs.** The concentrated sugar load is the main concern — habitual handfuls of dried figs can push glycemic load and contribute to dental wear. A small daily portion sits well inside normal patterns; a large bowl daily does not.

### pomegranate

**Pomegranate** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Pomegranate arils carry **punicalagins** and **ellagitannins** — a distinctive polyphenol family that gut bacteria convert into urolithin A, a compound increasingly studied for mitochondrial-aging markers. The arils also deliver vitamin C, potassium, and fibre. Longo's Mediterranean roots put pomegranate squarely on the seasonal autumn fruit list; Blueprint uses it less frequently but the polyphenol profile aligns with the protocol's emphasis.

**How to consume.** Eat the whole arils — seed included — for fibre and the full polyphenol payload. The juice carries most of the punicalagins but loses the fibre; unsweetened pomegranate juice (a small glass) is a reasonable rotation, but the whole arils are the better default. Sprinkle over grain salads, yoghurt, or roasted vegetables. The white pith is bitter and discarded.

**When to consume.** Seasonal — autumn through early winter in the Northern Hemisphere. Daily fruit slot during the season.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. The broader ellagitannin/polyphenol gap noted for berries applies.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Pomegranate juice can interact with some medications metabolised via the CYP3A4 pathway — a clinician question if you're on chronic medications.

### watermelon

**Watermelon** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Watermelon is roughly 92% water with a useful electrolyte profile (potassium, magnesium) and a distinctive carotenoid — **lycopene** — at concentrations rivalling cooked tomato. It also carries **citrulline**, an amino acid associated with vascular and exercise-recovery markers in small trials. Neither Blueprint nor Longo names it explicitly, but it sits comfortably in both patterns as a summer hydration and fruit slot.

**How to consume.** Eat fresh, ideally chilled, in generous wedges during hot weather. The flesh closest to the rind carries more citrulline; the deep red centre is more lycopene-dense. Frozen cubes work in smoothies. Pair with feta and mint for a classic Mediterranean salad.

**When to consume.** Seasonal — summer. A daily fruit slot during the season; the high water content makes it a useful hot-weather food.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category specific to watermelon. The broader lycopene/carotenoid gap noted for tomato applies — diets chronically low in red/orange produce miss out on the carotenoid family.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Very large single servings can act as a mild diuretic given the water and potassium load.

### raisin

**Raisin** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Raisins are dried grapes — they concentrate the potassium, iron, boron, and polyphenols of the fresh fruit as water leaves. They're a useful traditional iron source in plant-leaning diets and a quick-energy food for endurance work. Longo's pattern includes them sparingly, typically as a sweet accent in grain dishes or as the paired sweet with nuts.

**How to consume.** Small handfuls, ideally paired with nuts to blunt the sugar curve. Add to oats, grain salads, tagines, or baked goods. Soak briefly in warm water, wine, or rum if they've dried out hard. Look for unsulfured varieties when possible — the brown ones rather than the pale golden ones.

**When to consume.** Sparingly. A small daily handful is fine in Longo's pattern, inside the eating window. The fresh grape is a different proposition — eat that more freely in season.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category specific to raisins.

**Excess signs.** The concentrated sugar load is the main concern — habitual large handfuls push glycemic load and stick to teeth, contributing to dental wear. Small daily portions sit comfortably; bowl-a-day intake does not.

### cranberry-dried

**Cranberry (dried)** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** Fresh cranberries carry **proanthocyanidins** — a polyphenol family particularly studied for urinary-tract markers — alongside vitamin C and fibre. The dried form concentrates these compounds but also concentrates sugar, and most commercial dried cranberries are sweetened heavily because the raw fruit is too tart to eat unsweetened. Read the label: cranberries sweetened with apple juice or used unsweetened are the version to look for.

**How to consume.** Small portions, scattered into grain salads, oats, yoghurt, or baked goods. Pair with nuts to blunt the sugar curve. The unsweetened or apple-juice-sweetened versions are nutritionally cleaner; the standard sugar-sweetened "craisins" are closer to candy. Fresh or frozen cranberries are a different and more polyphenol-dense proposition when you can find them.

**When to consume.** Sparingly, as a flavour accent rather than a daily fruit slot.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category specific to cranberries.

**Excess signs.** The concentrated sugar load (especially in sweetened versions) is the main concern. Habitual large handfuls push glycemic load. No commonly-reported systemic excess pattern at culinary intake of the unsweetened form.

### berries-mixed

**Mixed berries** — _FRUIT_.

**Benefits.** A blend of blueberries, strawberries, blackberries, raspberries, and (sometimes) blackcurrants carries the full anthocyanin / ellagitannin / vitamin-C spread of the berry family in one handful. The rotation is the point: different pigments map to different polyphenol classes, and eating across the range covers more of the family than any single berry does. Blueprint leans on blueberries specifically, but a mixed-berry blend is the broader Longo-compatible move.

**How to consume.** Fresh in season; frozen out of season — the freezing process barely touches the polyphenol or vitamin content, and frozen mixed berries are usually cheaper and more polyphenol-dense than off-season fresh ones. Stir into yoghurt, oats, or salads; blend into smoothies; warm gently as a compote for pancakes. Pair with nuts or fat for slower sugar release.

**When to consume.** Daily. A cup of mixed berries a day is a reasonable default across both reference patterns.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category. The broader anthocyanin/polyphenol gap from diets chronically devoid of dark fruit tracks in cohort data with worse cognitive-aging markers.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### basil

**Basil** — _HERBS_.

**Benefits.** Basil is the aromatic backbone of Mediterranean cooking — its essential-oil profile (eugenol, linalool, methyl chavicol) carries antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activity in lab studies, and the leaves bring a small but real load of vitamin K and beta-carotene. Longo's recipes lean heavily on basil — pesto, tomato-basil pastas, finishing leaves on caprese-style dishes. Blueprint uses it less but accepts it freely. It's a culinary herb, not a daily required food, but the flavour payoff makes it one of the easier ways to keep fresh greens in the diet.

**How to consume.** Add at the end of cooking — basil's aromatics are volatile and the leaves blacken with sustained heat. Tear rather than chop with a steel blade if you want the brightest flavour (the metal accelerates oxidation). Classic preparations: pesto (raw, blended with pine nuts, garlic, olive oil, Parmesan), torn over tomato salads, finishing over soup. Store the stems in water like cut flowers, not in the fridge — basil blackens at low temperatures.

**When to consume.** Daily in summer when the plant is in season; otherwise as available. No specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — basil is a culinary herb, not an essential nutrient.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### parsley

**Parsley** — _HERBS_.

**Benefits.** Parsley is more nutritionally substantial than it looks — by weight it's a vitamin-K, vitamin-C, and folate concentrate, plus a flavonoid (apigenin, luteolin) source and an apiol/myristicin aromatic load. Italian flat-leaf parsley is the cooking variety; curly parsley is largely garnish. Longo's recipes use parsley generously — gremolata, tabbouleh-style salads, finishing on fish and beans. Treat it as a leafy green you happen to call an herb, not a sprig of garnish.

**How to consume.** Use the leaves in volume — a quarter-cup chopped flat-leaf parsley folded into a grain salad, a small bowl as the bulk of tabbouleh, a handful in gremolata. The stems are edible and carry most of the same compounds; chop them finely and use. Add at the end of cooking to keep the vitamin C intact. Pairs with lemon zest and garlic (the gremolata triplet).

**When to consume.** Daily is reasonable when in season. No specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category specific to parsley, but diets chronically low in dark leafy greens track in cohort data with worse vitamin-K and folate markers.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Concentrated parsley-seed oil is a different and traditionally medicinal preparation — not relevant at kitchen doses of the leaf.

### cilantro

**Cilantro / fresh coriander** — _HERBS_.

**Benefits.** Cilantro (the leaf; coriander is the seed) carries a distinctive aromatic-aldehyde profile that splits opinion sharply — a subset of people with a variant of the **OR6A2** olfactory receptor gene perceive it as soapy rather than herbal. For those who can stand it, the leaves bring vitamin K, vitamin C, and a polyphenol set (quercetin, kaempferol) plus some lab-study interest in heavy-metal chelation. It's the signature herb of Latin American and South Asian cooking and a useful rotation partner to parsley.

**How to consume.** Add at the end of cooking — the aromatics are volatile. Stems are edible and often more flavour-dense than leaves; chop and use. Classic pairings: lime + chili (the cilantro triplet), folded into bean dishes, scattered over fish tacos or curries. If you're cooking for someone with the OR6A2 variant, parsley or basil substitute reasonably.

**When to consume.** As available — no specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — cilantro is a culinary herb, not an essential nutrient.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### mint

**Mint** — _HERBS_.

**Benefits.** Mint's signature compound is **menthol**, which carries mild antimicrobial and digestive-soothing activity — the traditional after-meal mint tea of North Africa and the Middle East has a real basis in motility and gas relief. The leaves also deliver small amounts of vitamin A, vitamin C, and rosmarinic acid. Longo's Mediterranean roots use mint across yoghurt sauces, grain salads (tabbouleh), and infusions; Blueprint uses it less but accepts it freely.

**How to consume.** Tear or chop at the end of cooking — menthol is volatile and bruising releases more of it than clean knife cuts. Classic preparations: mint tea (fresh leaves steeped in hot water), tabbouleh (mint + parsley + bulgur + lemon), yoghurt sauces (tzatziki-style), pea-and-mint soups. Pair with lamb, peas, cucumber, watermelon, dark chocolate.

**When to consume.** After meals as tea is a traditional and reasonable habit, particularly for people prone to bloating. Otherwise as available.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — mint is a culinary herb, not an essential nutrient.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Concentrated peppermint oil is a different preparation and can lower lower-esophageal sphincter tone — relevant for reflux at therapeutic doses, not at culinary ones.

### rosemary-fresh

**Rosemary (fresh)** — _HERBS_.

**Benefits.** Rosemary carries **carnosic acid**, **carnosol**, and **rosmarinic acid** — a polyphenol/diterpene set studied for cognitive and anti-inflammatory markers. The aromatic oils (cineole, camphor, pinene) make rosemary one of the few herbs that genuinely strengthens with heat, which is why it's a roasting and grilling staple. Longo's Tuscan-leaning recipes use rosemary across roasted vegetables, beans, and focaccia; Blueprint uses it less frequently.

**How to consume.** Sprigs go in early with roasting vegetables, beans, or potatoes — heat coaxes the oils out rather than destroying them. Strip the needles from the stem and chop finely if you want them dispersed in the dish; whole sprigs are fished out before serving. Pair with olive oil, garlic, lemon, white beans. A small sprig is usually enough — the flavour is assertive.

**When to consume.** As available — no specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — rosemary is a culinary herb, not an essential nutrient.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### sage

**Sage** — _HERBS_.

**Benefits.** Sage carries **thujone**, **camphor**, and **rosmarinic acid** — a polyphenol/terpene profile with the strongest cognitive-marker literature among the common kitchen herbs (small trials note effects on memory tasks at culinary-adjacent doses). The flavour is assertive and slightly bitter; a little goes a long way. Longo's Tuscan corpus uses sage with white beans, pumpkin, and brown butter; Blueprint uses it sparingly.

**How to consume.** Tear or chop fresh leaves at the end of cooking, or fry whole leaves briefly in olive oil or butter for a crispy garnish (a classic over pumpkin gnocchi or white beans). Dry sage is stronger per gram than fresh and works well in stuffings and rubs. Pair with white beans, winter squash, pork, brown butter, lemon.

**When to consume.** As available — no specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — sage is a culinary herb, not an essential nutrient.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Concentrated sage essential oil and prolonged high-dose sage-tea regimens carry thujone-load concerns at therapeutic-dose levels — not relevant at kitchen doses of the leaf.

### herb-mix

**Mixed culinary herbs** — _HERBS_.

**Benefits.** "Mixed herbs" in the Mediterranean kitchen usually means a dried blend of **oregano, thyme, basil, and rosemary** — the Italian seasoning / herbes de Provence pattern. Each contributes its own essential-oil profile (carvacrol from oregano, thymol from thyme, eugenol from basil, carnosic acid from rosemary), and the blend as a whole carries one of the highest antioxidant densities of any dried-pantry item per gram. Longo's pasta sauces, bean stews, and roasted vegetables lean on this blend reflexively; Blueprint uses Italian seasoning across many of its savoury recipes.

**How to consume.** Dried blends are added early — they need heat and time to bloom, and they tolerate sustained cooking far better than the fresh equivalents. Stir into soffrito with the onions, into tomato sauce as it reduces, or onto vegetables before roasting. A teaspoon goes a long way. Store in a dark jar; dried herbs lose potency after about a year on the shelf — replace annually for the cleanest flavour.

**When to consume.** As available — no specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category — mixed herbs are culinary, not an essential nutrient.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake.

### chickpea

**Chickpea** — _LEGUMES_GRAINS_PASTA_.

**Benefits.** The chickpea is the workhorse of both reference patterns. Longo treats it as one of the cornerstone legumes of the longevity diet — high plant-protein, high fibre, low glycemic load, with a folate, iron, and manganese profile that anchors the Mediterranean-pescatarian frame. Blueprint relies on chickpea flour for the **nutty-flour bowl** that's a recurring breakfast / lunch base in the protocol, and on whole chickpeas for stews and salads. Both corpuses lean on it heavily.

**How to consume.** Dried chickpeas, soaked overnight and simmered, hold the firmest texture and the cleanest flavour; canned chickpeas (rinsed) are a faster default and nutritionally close. Pair with olive oil, lemon, tahini, cumin, parsley (the hummus axis) or with tomato and rosemary (the Tuscan stew axis). Chickpea flour (farinata, socca, the Blueprint nutty-flour bowl) is a useful gluten-free flour with the same protein and fibre profile. Roasted chickpeas make a crunchy snack with a protein-fat profile better than most savoury snacks.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine in Longo's pattern. A cup of cooked chickpeas a day is well inside the longevity-diet frame. Inside the eating window; no specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a chickpea-specific deficiency, but diets chronically low in legumes broadly track in cohort data with worse fibre, folate, and plant-protein markers — and with the cardiovascular and metabolic patterns those gaps imply.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Sudden large increases in legume intake can produce transient bloating as the gut microbiome adapts — ramp up gradually if you're new to them.

### lentil

**Lentil** — _LEGUMES_GRAINS_PASTA_.

**Benefits.** Lentils are the fastest-cooking common legume and one of the most plant-protein- and iron-dense — green, brown, black (beluga), and red varieties each carry slightly different textures but a similar nutritional core. They're a Longo staple — the Italian lentil-and-rice (riso e lenticchie) and lentil soup traditions sit squarely inside the longevity diet. Lentils also carry a useful polyphenol load in the seed coat (more in the dark varieties).

**How to consume.** No soak required — rinse and simmer. Green and brown lentils hold their shape (good for salads and stews); red lentils dissolve into a creamy texture (good for dal and soup); black beluga lentils hold their shape and look striking on the plate. Pair with cumin, coriander, garlic, lemon, and olive oil; squeeze lemon at the table to help non-heme iron absorption.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine. A bowl of lentil soup or stew sits comfortably inside both reference patterns. Inside the eating window; no specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a lentil-specific deficiency. The broader pattern noted for chickpeas — diets chronically low in legumes track with worse fibre, folate, and plant-protein markers — applies.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Same gut-adaptation note as chickpeas — ramp up gradually if legumes are new.

### black-bean

**Black bean** — _LEGUMES_GRAINS_PASTA_.

**Benefits.** Black beans carry the standard legume profile (plant protein, fibre, folate, iron) plus an anthocyanin load in the dark seed coat that puts them at the polyphenol-dense end of the bean family. They're the staple bean of Latin American kitchens — black beans and rice (a complete-protein pairing) is one of the more nutritionally complete one-pot foods in common rotation. Longo accepts black beans within the broader legume slot; Blueprint uses them less frequently than chickpeas.

**How to consume.** Dried beans, soaked overnight and simmered with bay and garlic, hold the cleanest flavour and texture; canned (rinsed) is the faster default. Pair with cumin, oregano, lime, cilantro, chili (the Latin American axis) or with rice for the classic complete-protein pairing. Black-bean soup, refried black beans, and black-bean salads all keep the seed-coat polyphenols on the plate.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine within the broader legume rotation. Inside the eating window; no specific timing.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a black-bean-specific deficiency. The broader low-legume pattern applies.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Same gut-adaptation note as the other legumes.

### dried-bean-mixed

**Dried beans (mixed / cannellini-borlotti)** — _LEGUMES_GRAINS_PASTA_.

**Benefits.** "Mixed dried beans" in Longo's corpus usually means the Italian white-and-pink axis — **cannellini** (white kidney), **borlotti** (cranberry bean), and sometimes **lamon** beans — that anchors Tuscan and northern-Italian bean cookery. The nutritional core is the standard legume profile (plant protein, fibre, folate, iron, manganese) with subtle texture and flavour differences across varieties. The point of cooking them mixed is the slow, layered texture of a bean stew that no single variety achieves alone.

**How to consume.** Soak overnight, then simmer slowly with bay, sage, garlic, and olive oil — the Tuscan way is to cook them gently for hours until the skins are soft but intact. Pair with rosemary, sage, tomato, kale, farro. Pasta e fagioli (pasta and beans) is the canonical use; ribollita and minestrone fold them into vegetable stews. Save the cooking liquid — it carries flavour and starch that thickens whatever you're making.

**When to consume.** Daily is fine within Longo's pattern. Inside the eating window; no specific timing. The classic Italian context is lunch or early evening, with bread and olive oil.

**Deficiency signs.** Not a deficiency category specific to mixed beans. The broader low-legume pattern applies — diets chronically low in legumes track with worse fibre, folate, and plant-protein markers.

**Excess signs.** No commonly-reported excess pattern at culinary intake. Same gut-adaptation note as the other legumes — ramp up gradually if they're new to the diet.
